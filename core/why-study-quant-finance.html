Why Study Quant Finance?

Home » Core » Why Study Quant Finance?
Created: 2019-07-18 • Modified: 2019-08-02

I highly recommend anyone interested in Computer Science, Data Science or AI to at least causally follow the world of FinTech and Quantitative Finance. Even if you are not interested in the world of finance or econometrics itself, the world of Finance is generally ahead of every other field both conceptually and technologically. This present domain experts outside FinTech numerous opportunities to arbitrage on best tools and practices to enhance research and learning in their own fields.

Two of the best books I’ve read in recent months have been on Quant Finance. This is my multi-dimensional definition of ‘best’ in that these books combine mathematically interesting and cutting-edge techniques that are ruthlessly focused on practice and measurable outcomes. The intellectual economy and theoretical beauty explains why so many math and physics minds are attracted to the abstract challenges presented by quantitative finance.

“Machine Learning for Finance” by Jaanes Klaas is unique in that it covers both an unusually broad range of topics including the frontiers of research. While most trade books tend towards the narrow application of specific skills or mastery of a particular tool, Klaas gives an expansive intellectual framework that connects many topics in logical and insightful ways. The cost of this approach is that you either have to have familiarity with basic math concepts or be able to supplement gaps in your knowledge. The reward is that you walk away with a more integrated and universal understanding of AI/ML by converting unknown-unknowns into either known-knowns or at least known-unknowns.

I highly recommended this for those with a core undergrad math background in linear algebra, calc and stats but no background in econ or quant finance. It will help you connect the dots between ML/AI theory, practice and research that otherwise requires much more ground work. One of the best aspects of the books are the clear code examples in both Python and R, inclusion of key libraries and commentary on important trade-offs. He also intentionally avoids repetitive examples of financial time-series to emphasize the cross-disciplinary application of these techniques which was an interesting twist.

“Advances in Financial Machine Learning” by Marcos Lopez de Prado is a very different type of book on Quantitative Finance. This books is more theoretical and in-depth on the mathematics but lighter on code. What is stunning about this book is that Lopez de Prado reveals models and techniques that are sophisticated, convincingly practical and thus rarely shared openly in the FinTech world. The book also demystifies FinTech as less alchemy and more a continual innovation pipeline diligently integrating across domain experts, model theorist, programmers and HPC engineers. It’s not complete and raises as many questions in my mind as it answers, but it’s the most honest and believable account of how quant finance sausage is really made.

Again, this is not to necessarily advocate for a career in quant finance. Rather, it is an invitation to explore and learn from some of the best practices and sharpest minds tackling grappling with some of the most difficult intellectual problems in CS – many of which can be translated and applied to important problems outside quant finance.

For example, last year I advised a student in our AI for the Humanities course who was passionate about environmental issues. She used financial regime detection methods to discover shifts in public opinion on environmental spending over several decades. Her research using Bayesian statistical packages more commonly found in FinTech revealed several plausible yet surprising scenarios correlating world events with shifts in public attitudes.
