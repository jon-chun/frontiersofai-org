Created: 2019-01-09 • Modified: 2025-09-10

Last week we explored the many facets of data–in other words, our “input,” both for humans (perception and memory) or computers (bits/bytes, sampling, compression). This week, we look at processing that data, again comparing computers and humans. We begin by exploring the long history of attempts to describe, systematize and automate cognition. The origins of computer science can be said to date back to Aristotle, and the field has been heavily influenced by math, logic and linguistics. You may be surprised to learn how influential philosophers have been to the field!

In terms of code, we’ll learn how Computer Science represents and implements one familiar aspect of cognition: explicit algorithmic thinking. We take a brief break from DataCamp (the only time this semester) to complete a chapter in a more traditional computer science online textbook (“Thinking Like a Computer Scientist” on Runestone). We’ll also study specific popular algorithms for common tasks (e.g. sort and search) as well as general patterns of computation (e.g. iteration and recursion). 

How similar is algorithmic thinking to our own human processing? Is our mind really like a computer or do we process information differently?

We also highlight the unique role of consciousness and the ways in which self-awareness and qualitative experience–the particular way you experience the world as “you”– is (or is not) an essential part of our processing system. How closely are intelligence and consciousness linked? Can consciousness be explained mathematically, materially, or spiritually? Is consciousness a fundamental property of the universe like information, matter and energy? A material process of our biology? An emergent property of a complex system’s recursive, non-linear neuronal pathways? A mathematical formula? A way of integrating information? Our conclusion has implications for whether we will be able to replicate consciousness in our machines. While theories of consciousness used to be the domain of religion and philosophy, we now have computer scientists, neuroscientists and even a few physicists joining the debate. Of course, the larger question is undoubtedly: should we even try?
