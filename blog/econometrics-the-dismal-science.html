Econometrics: The Dismal Science

Home » Blog » Econometrics: The Dismal Science
Posted by jonchunMay 23, 2019

Update (6/3/2018): ‘Market Prediction’ in this post embeds the concept of ‘Market Timing’. General predictions without precise values tied to exact timeframes are more tractable but difficult to build a profitable trading firm upon. As the 2008 asset bubble and subsequent financial crisis illustrated: ‘The market can remain irrational longer than you can stay solvent‘ (excluding speculating firms engaging too large to fail).

The first mainstream personal computer was arguably the Apple II which went on sale in 1977. So before I could get into computers, I had a few years of my youth in Iowa to obsesses over other things.

The two obsessions I remember best from these years are attempting to read the set of Encyclopedia Britannica we owned and sitting in the non-fiction stacks at the local public library reading anything related to history. This paid big dividends with peak Trivial Pursuit in the mid-1980s and whenever Jeopardy is played in a hotel lobby.

So I was surprised to learn that I had misunderstood the description of Economics as ‘The Dismal Science’. As a young STEM person aware of the faux-Noble in Economics, I must have confidently parsed the phrase ‘The Dismal Science’ as ‘Pseudo-Science’ or ‘Not Very Scientific Science’ or ‘Guesswork Borrowing from the Legitimacy of Science’ without enough doubt to warrant looking up the actual etymology.

Only two weeks ago I learned the semantic stress in this phrase is on the word ‘Dismal’ rather than ‘Science’. It turns out Victorian historian Thomas Carlyle sarcastically coined the term ‘The Dismal Science’ for the very pessimistic outlooks embodied in economists’ theories of the time like the Malthusian Trap and Ricardo’s Iron Law of Wages.

My first real job out of college was working in the financial district of Tokyo. This experience only reinforced my initial understanding of ‘The Dismal Science’. Our office was staffed with two types of economists. The first wined and dined officials from local big banks, BOJ, and MITI government departments to write original commentary on local sentiments. The second type of economists jockied Excel spreadsheets all day searching the template graphs for telltale ‘J’ and ‘X’ shapes (e.g. technical analysis or market fundamentals). To the rational observer or even street skeptic, it seemed anything but ‘science’ or ‘predictive’. Why else would these economists be selling their lives by the hour to feed the voracious appetites of Bloomberg terminals rather than printing money themselves by arbitraging on such insights if accurate?

Observing and talking with various individuals in or around the finance game over the years did little to disabuse me of my interpretation of ‘The Dismal Science’. In particular, the Wall Street quants I’ve known or heard on the matter readily acknowledge the difficulty if not the impossibility of mathematically consistently predicting markets. This stands in stark contrast to the near-uniform assumptions embedded into virtually every financial pundit tasked with generating just-so ex post facto explanations for any market movement (with a heads I win, tails you lose accountability).

The only consistently successful strategies to pass the smell test seemed to be ones involving unfair competitive advantages in information, speed and size to arbitrage similarly disadvantaged counterparties like small investors or dumb institutional investors like pension funds. Having your execs rotate through the US Treasury Secretary office, front running large institutional orders with high-speed networks, proprietary datasets or using instant market information with immense order size to make market movements rather than follow them seemed like the best odds. It’s hard to confidently suss out since so much of information around this industry is confidential.

Last year I came across a book by Marcos Lopez de Prado titled ‘Advances in Financial Machine Learning‘ that sufficiently explained the operations of a quantitative finance to shift my mind. It argues that quant finance is more about marshaling and coordinating a variety of sophisticated resources to develop, synchronize and continually evolve new ideas like an intellectual factory with perishable models. The Hollywood ideal of the heroic lone mathematical genius is more realistically fleshed out with a team of similarly high-performing HPC, software and financial domain experts to efficiently build, sustain, monitor and quickly update this pipeline of financial models.

No doubt a lot of money has been poured into hiring up quants on Wall Street since financialization began in earnest in the 1980s with cause. Although there are likely many niches where sophisticated models can prove out returns, I suspect for now quants are highly valued for reasons other than present market-crushing returns. In general, quants are very clever and clever individuals usually outperform at any given task. Even small advantages in the hyper-competitive world of Wall Street can be leveraged into immense gains. Finally, quants provide insurance against the future that may well become more rational or predictable – no one can afford to be left behind.

As algorithmic investing, trading and business forecasting inevitably becomes more prevalent, the echos of such computational approaches should create a stronger signal among chaotic noise of human economic activity. Quantitative methods as common practices will create their own reality like a self-fulfilling prophecy in feedback fashion. Interesting times ahead perhaps.

Here are several other resources I’ve found useful in advising my econ student in potential quant-related projects for my class:

  1. https://quantecon.org/ – Teach yourself quantitative finance in Python or Julia from a Nobel Prize-winning economist.
  2. https://otexts.com/fpp2/ Go a bit more in-depth on time-series analysis – the foundational aggregate datatype of economic analysis.
  3. https://www.quantopian.com/ – Test your skill and luck in a low-stakes quant model competition.
  4. Join the Dark Side and get a job as a quant

A word of caution for the idealistic and pure-hearted in the form of a slide from my “Programming Humanity” course during the week we discuss Modeling.
