Synthetic Biology: iGem

Home » Blog » Synthetic Biology: iGem
Posted by jonchunApril 23, 2019

Our Programming Humanity course concludes with introductions to several high-level technologies and computational approaches in areas poised to significantly impact humanity: Robotics, Bioinformatics and Artificial Intelligence. Each of these areas is a vast discipline unto itself with ethical consequences ranging from potential widespread automation/unemployment and lethal autonomous weapons to biological engineering and neo-eugenics.

In addition to reviewing the fundamentals of molecular biology and genetics, we introduce the tools and techniques of various -omics like Illumina Dye Sequencing and CRISPR-Cas9 targeted DNA editing. Perhaps most interesting from a long-term general knowledge perspective is our review of the emerging field of Synthetic Biology (bottom up) and Systems Biology (top down). These relatively new disciplines attempt to bring efficiency, consistency and rigor to the notoriously complex and fuzzy world of biology by incorporating traditional Engineering practices, methods and mathematical models.

This particular Engineering Systems approach to biology (and -omics fields in general) was perhaps most famously pioneered with MIT’s Biobricks. The impressive results from grafting this ‘Engineering Systems’ thinking onto biology is perhaps best exemplified by the annual International Genetic Engineered Machines (iGem) Competition. iGem in turn is based upon the highly successful First LEGO League International Robotics Competitions which combines scholarship, community service and innovation/entrepreneurship in a coopertition style tournament framework.

As someone with both Electrical Engineering/Computer Science and Molecular Biology/Genetics/Medical background this confluence of these superficially disparate fields is extremely exciting. The fusion represents a maturation of the Biological Sciences that brings much needed theory, opens vast new opportunities for innovation and possesses an intellectually elegant aesthetic akin to what mathematicians/physicists describe in uncovering an unexpected symmetry, simplicity or universality in various formalisms. From the intractable complexity and inherently large statistical errors in biology (likely arising more out of unknown unknowns than experimental errors) to the traditional hand-wavy practice of medicine, I felt an odd duck out in medical school several decades ago. I was even dragged into the Deans office for a personal reprimand when I organized a student meeting to form a group to quantitatively analyze various alternative medical practices that were rising in popularity at the time. I was more or less informed that such inquiry did not reflect well upon the institution and to cease. Today such approaches are increasingly mainstream under the names of ‘Health Data Analytics’, ‘Functional Medicine’ and ‘Quantitative Medicine’. Connections that seem heretical only two decades ago are finding scientific grounding like the connection between gut flora and biofilm compositions with various disease states.

The seamless integration and initial impressive results of this Biology + Engineering = System Biology is incredibly compelling. As with the topics of Robotics last week and AI next week, experience and knowledge train one to cautiously temper the current optimistic future outlooks for Systems Biology. Early wins and low hanging fruit belie the much broader set of underlying challenges and complexities involved. Solving and generalizing more unstructured than those currently narrowly defined within most labs will involve more than a linear scaling of complexity and effort I suspect.

By definition the early wins will be the easiest. The question is if the path of development for Systems Biology will unfold more slowly like NLP at Dartmouth after 1956 or rapidly like DNNs after ImageNet in 2012. Biology at the -omics level up is orders of magnitude more difficult than NLP yet in many situations more forgiving of individual anomalies given the statistical natures of some big data problems involved. My current prior is that the statistical nature of many biological processes means that many practical innovations will be realized shortly although exacting applications with lower tolerances for error may prove elusive. We will need to methodically fill in the gaps between the layers of abstraction we somewhat understand and the emergent properties and causal chains between them that are poorly understood.

Still, I can help wonder what kind of world the next generation will live in if things continue along an even a dampened trajectory. I found this 2017 presentation by the William & Mary runner up team an incredibly beautiful meld of Engineering Sciences and the Biological Science.
