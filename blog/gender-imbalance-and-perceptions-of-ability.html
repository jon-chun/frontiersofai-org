STEM Gender Imbalance and Perceptions of Ability

Home » Blog » STEM Gender Imbalance and Perceptions of Ability
Posted by jonchunJune 27, 2019

Here is an interesting chart I’ve seen repeatedly in my recent research into the current trends in Computer Science education.

I find this graph particularly interesting since I’ve studied, worked and researched at both ends of the spectrum of so-called “hard” (CS, Engineering, Physics) and “soft” (Neuroscience, Molecular Biology and Biochemistry) sciences. The statistical outliers in this linear regression are Computer Science and Earth Science (relatively) biased towards males and Biochemistry, Molecular Biology and Evolutionary Biology (relatively) biased towards females.

From my experiences, the biggest opportunities I can see for improvements here are in Engineering and Computer Science. I have a pretty high threshold for watching paint dry in search abstract intellectual payoffs and even I found my education in these fields largely tedious and soulless. This is one of the reasons why I segued into the Life Sciences and Medicine. The sad fact is that CS and Engineering do not have to manifest as boring route exercises. They are fundamentally creative enterprises at the intersections of intellectually fascinating traditions like Math and Physics as well as increasingly humanistic concerns central to Philosophy, Psychology and Literature.

There are so many opportunities, especially with the rapid pace of progress and availability in technologies like AI, to more directly connect CS and Engineering to wider human concerns and meaningful social good. Innovative new approaches have been designed by a wide variety of places like Olin College, Harvard University and Harvey Mudd College. Unfortunately, change comes very slowly in academia and the vast majority of institutions are more individualist and incremental in nature in responding to this opportunity.

The counterfactual for the sizable headroom to improve outcomes in Computer Science can be seen in the chart above. Both CS and Math are outliers but in opposite directions. However, below the college level instruction in both Math and Computer Science are generally recognized as similarly poorly executed. Many purists view elementary/HS education teaching a shallow and unappealing parody of true math focusing on mechanical calculations and manipulations of symbols by blind ritual more than the beautiful universal truths that drive maths at college level and beyond (c.f. Lockhart’s Lament).

Many would agree that Computer Science is similarly poorly taught at the elementary/HS levels (if taught at all). It’s telling that, perhaps far more than any other STEM discipline, the most proficient CS students entering college are self-taught. However, whereas instruction in Math at the college-level and beyond suddenly become far more engaging and true to its roots instruction in Computer Science changes very little at this crossover point. The difference in topics, practice and pedagogy is largely still focused on narrow technical specializations: introductory programming, data structures, algorithms, etc.

The good news is that this is slowly changing. The biggest driver to these changes are the need to find new approaches to teaching CS that is welcoming to a more diverse set of students. This is especially pressing in a world where computational thinking is increasingly a fundamental form of literacy required to thrive in the modern world. I discuss the details at length in an upcoming paper.

As an end note, I still can’t explain the positioning of the “Biochemistry” data point. It suffers much of the minutia, tedium and complexity of the less successful subjects and has the furthest intellectual connection to the innate humanist appeal of the life sciences. I enjoyed it as an intellectual exercise like Organic Chemistry, but I suspect that OChem would score even below Chemistry in the diagram above. On the other hand, Biochemistry did seem to hold more secrets to the mystery of life than either Chem or OChem. I remember studying the Krebs Cycle in undergrad and grad school as a sort of meditative reflection on a mandala.
