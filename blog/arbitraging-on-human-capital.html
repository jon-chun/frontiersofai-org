Arbitraging on Human Capital

Home » Blog » Arbitraging on Human Capital
Posted by jonchunMay 25, 2019

One of the major transformations I’ve witnessed over the last several decades is how technology has been able to assist in creating incredibly efficient markets in everything. Since the 1980s of nearly everything from housing to education to health helped has been increasingly financialized to reduce risk, more efficiently allocate capital and better discover optimal pricing. A carry-on effect has also transformed the way we view, identify, nuture and promote human talent.

After WWII militaries became increasingly reliant on technology from jet fighters to missile delivery systems to satellite communications. Fueled by the US-USSR cold war and perhaps peaking with 1960s space race, there was a concerted effort to test, identify and promote highly talented students in STEM on a large scale. Potentiated by speculative or specialized military applications, expanding and coordinated globalized basic research and growing commercial opportunities the demand for human capital in STEM seems perpetually unmet.

Even occasional downturns like those in the military aerospace industries in SoCal and Texas supercollider often simply reallocated STEM talent from one industry to another. SoCal is now a major computer tech hub and many PhDs in Physics simply retooled as Wall Street quants, tech developers or even leaders in entirely new fields like genomics.

At the same time, the global pool of human talent has rapidly expanded and become more mobile. Over the past four decades, the countries of the former USSR, China, India, and Eastern Europe have flooded the market with massive amounts of world-class talent once isolated behind the political or economic barriers. Increased international travel, education, and work have created an ever-more efficient globalized brain drain from anywhere in the world into academic and tech hubs like Silicon Valley, Cambridge, Beijing, London and Berlin.

The result has been both a growing demand for tech talent along with rising expectations from those workers. What was once a birthright of secure upper-middle-class life for the average American software engineer in Sunnyvale, CA has now become a life-long challenge to stay competitive with globalized competition. As reflected in the nationalities of PhD graduates in the hard sciences from America’s top universities, many capable native-born Americans are rationally opting for more secure and lucrative careers in fields like economics or medicine.

For example, my grandfather-in-law was born on a farm in rural Ohio, minimally educated and worked his entire life keeping the printing presses operating for the Cleveland Plain Dealer. One of his friends from Cleveland who migrated and achieved some success in San Francisco claimed his man was the brightest person he ever met. It’s hard to image with standardized testing, scholarships and the Internet that this person today would have spent his life teaching himself how to repair printing presses.

Another more contemporaneous example comes from my experience leading a Kenyon team at a Ohio5 Hackathon recently. In talking with student members from one of the nearby elite small liberal arts colleges they explained how they suddenly lost half their CS faculty a few weeks into the semester. It turns out Google was monitoring their search activity and certain key terms triggered a popup dialog box to appear on their screens. The dialog box asked if they would be interested in working for Google, invited them to take a simple quiz and lead to job offers so good they couldn’t turn them down.

Finally, perhaps a more speculative example exists in the form of my young kids. Their elementary, middle and HS are standardized on Chromebooks which have potential spy to every test they take, what score they got, which questions they got wrong, how much time they spent on each question, where their cursor and eye gaze lingered, etc. They spend most of their screentime at home on Android devices which send analytic data back to Google throughout the day. Family members have sequenced the most important SNPs in our family trees with 23andme which some companies are aggressively seeking to correlate with human characteristics like cognitive, behavioral and physical traits. Image this all these data feeds informing an AI searching for smaller and smaller arbitrage opportunities to discover human talent. If they are lucky, they just may receive an invitation to interview with an AI.

Without an inside line on personal information like Google has access to it’s become increasingly difficult to discover undervalued properties. About a decade ago my last Silicon Valley startup arbitraged on the inefficiencies of academia in selecting, filtering and training extremely talented graduates and post-docs in Theoretical Physics and then leaving them without any next step in academia. Now, there is a broad realization of this opportunity as even my undergraduate Physics students seem to have some of the best job opportunities.

Still, there are a number of opportunities to mine for human talent that big organizations cannot algorithmically recognize and automatically capture at scale. The big three that immediately come to mind are age, location and non-standard candidates. The first is fairly obvious given age discrimination in tech. The distributed organization is more of an organizational, management and scale problem although a growing number of innovative companies are solving this. As the cost and quality of living diverge in major tech hubs, tech workers and investors tire of seeing more of their money go to rent-seekers instead of into product or lifestyle.

And finally, the non-traditional but talented individual who obviously will do well despite sparse historical datasets or typical resume-job pattern matching. I know a 50yr+ former Classics Professor with no prior programming experience who is now thriving as a Java consultant after only a 3mo boot camp, so maybe the window is closing on this arbitrage opportunity as well. Still, this is probably the least tapped vein and the biggest opportunity as fundamental shifts in technology redefine and expand the definition of human talent and the economic value thereof (see my upcoming research paper).
