"Artificial" Artificial Intelligence

Home » Blog » "Artificial" Artificial Intelligence
Posted by jonchunMarch 11, 2019

With the hype around AI and Machine Learning there is bandwagon effect that a lot of AI startup, products and projects are more “Artificial” than “Artificial Intelligence”.

For example, libraries like XGBoost and LightGBM dominate Kaggle data science competitions using numerical datasets. In addition, the underlying simplicity of these approaches (e.g. variations of ensembles of simpler models like decision trees) have extremely high performance characteristics and better explainability lacking in more complex and cutting edge AI approaches like Deep Neural Networks.

In addition, simple (or not so simple) linear regression also tends to be a first approximation model that are good enough in many real-world applications outside FinTech, HealthTech and GAFA/Silicon Valley. Often the limited delta improvement over a well crafted linear regression model by experienced domain experts does not justify the cost/time/difficulty in implementing more complex models. This is where such options even exist in terms of human talent, data and infrastructure which are in short supply today.

Finally, much of what at first glance seems AI magic spinning straw into gold often turns out to be the old magician’s trick of misdirection and letting the listener’s/reporter’s imagination fill in the blanks. For example, breathless reports of AI-generated sports/financial newspaper articles, annual reports and legal research turn out to be a lot of Eliza-like case statements, SQL/RegEx pattern matches and decades-old traditional Information Retrieval techniques in very narrow NLP niches.

Here is a good presentation that reminds us critically consider the nature of every situation to evaluate when the old ways are still the best ways: When do you use ML vs a Rules-based System by Soup Ranjan at QCon.ai 2018.

Of course, the newfangled AI has made impressive strides and is absolutely provides unparalleled performance on a small and growing set of problem domains like dealing with unstructured data, certain underspecified problems and agents that need to learn in highly complex and variable environments. Still, simpler statistical models, GOFAI and other heritage approaches are still surprisingly effective for a large number problems today and should in the model toolkit.
