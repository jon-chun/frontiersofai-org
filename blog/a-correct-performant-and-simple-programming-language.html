It seems we've only progressed from a "pick 1" to "pick 1.5" of the three listed in this title over the past few decades. I attribute the +0.5 to some of the promising technologies presented in Dan Pittman's amazingly lucid presentation below (unfortunately not quite ready for large-scale commercialization).

When I was a student or early in my career I could afford to be carefree and view programming as a creative endeavor like playing with simple old-school LEGOs or puzzling through old Martin Gardner columns in SciAm. I even enjoyed exploring the byzantine alleys ways of inelegant languages/DSLs like the original releases of JavaScript, Perl, Oracle PL/SQL, RegEx.

Later, when I took on the responsibilities as a CIO, CTO, and Director of Development in Silicon Valley at companies that handled sensitive insurance claim data, managed high-volumes of private/anonymous transactions over the Internet or engineered custom VPN network appliances my attitudes changed. I wanted systems, programming environments and development pipelines that were correct, performant and easy to learn/use.

Things have gotten better. The best tools are generally some degree of easier to use, scalable, feature-rich and FOSS (Linux, Visual Studio, github libraries, PostgreSQL, Apache Projects, TensorFlow, Tableau). Programming languages offer a better choice of more powerful (Python), secure (Rust), reliable (Elixir/Erlang) and scalable (Scala/Haskell) alternatives. Development practices, infrastructures and costs have vastly improved with cloud service providers, price/performance drops, agile development, github versioning, automated CI, TDD, etc.

However, as software continues to eat the world with ever more complex and high-stakes systems the biggest shortcoming is in ensuring software correctness. Currently, this is largely done only in a small set of critical domains like avionics and healthcare instrumentation. The vast majority of software is more a spray and pray sort of affair with a minimal set of test-cases only slightly enhanced with variations like fuzzing and test-driven development that hopefully catches the most common potential errors and edge cases.
