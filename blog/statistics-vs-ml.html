Statistics vs ML

Home » Blog » Statistics vs ML
Posted by jonchunSeptember 12, 2019

Before I taught the first iteration of our “AI for the Humanities” course a colleague asked “Isn’t that just statistics?” There wasn’t time to address this question in the hallway at the time, but I’ve dwelt on it a lot since.

The similarities between Statistics and AI is akin to the similarities between statistics, physics and engineering. From one perspective, all three fields are just applied math of sorts. Despite this common base in math, the assumptions, working abstraction levels and goals are very different between these three fields just as they are very different between statistics and AI (even as AI relies a particular subset statistics and math). R-bloggers’s May 2016 post “What’s the Difference Between Machine Learning, Statistics and Data Mining” by Sharp Sight Labs gives a good first pass on this controversy.

Perhaps the key difference is found in practice: proficiency in one field rarely translates smoothly to the other fields. Thus we have the Data Scientist ‘Unicorn’ for the rare individual proficient in programming and maths/statistics (as well as visualizations, communications, rigorous scientific method along with domain expertise). Jeremy Howard, author of one of the most popular hands-on AI courses at fast.ai, has mentioned students with a background in programming generally excel performance and innovation compared to backgrounds in other areas like statistics (although to be fair the quality of Silicon Valley area programmers motivated to take his class is more exceptional than those from other categories).

In practice, most working data scientists I suspect are using relatively simple small-scale statistical models like linear regression, decision trees and AR time-series models. Sometime these bread-and-butter techniques are augmented with more sophisticated refinements like GLMs, gradient boosted tree ensembles, and ARIMA. Still, I’m always surprised to learn how much mileage these basic methods can deliver in real-world situations. Much of this has to do with the fact that simplicity, reliability, explainability, regulations and ease of implementation are much bigger concerns in industry. Media headlines distort this reality by highlighting advances at the edges of theory in Academia and practice in Silicon Valley. Basic risk-reward calculations also bias industry toward simpler and stabler approaches to run core business operations. Still, as we see ML/AI progress and mature it’s inevitable that intelligently adopting reliable and higher performance methods will become an essential aspect of remaining competitive.

A crude yet functional distinction is that the traditional statistics-first ML approaches deal with relatively small-scale explainable data models while an AI-first approach like Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) deal with large-scale black-box data models powered by big-data. The statistics-first approach often involves close collaboration between domain experts and statisticians to carefully handcraft human wisdom into the model. The AI-first approach operates on much larger models govern by general rules-of-thumb, experience and rapid iterative exploration. The rules in the AI approach are automatically created from the data itself which can involve learning millions of model parameters from billions of data points. The statistics-first approach directly draws on statistical and domain expertise while the ML/AI approach indirectly surfaces latent structure and relationships hidden within large datasets. Perhaps the biggest difference is the surprising result that the big-data shotgun end-to-end black-box ML/AI-first approach significantly outperforms the more intentional specialized hand-crafted statistics-first approaches in more and more real-world applications.

Long-term trends in greater computing power, new and more efficient algorithms and massive datasets have enabled Deep Neural Nets (DNNs) to return from exile since 2012 and eclipse previous complex traditional statistical models. In domains with large, unstructured and noisy data like voice recognition, image recognition, and natural language processing traditional statistical approaches have largely been abandoned as dead ends. Even more structured tabular numerical data is apparently better analyzed with DNNs than the traditional statistical approaches. Jeremy Howard (one-time President and #1 competitor of Kaggle for two straight years) claims in his newest fast.ai course v3 that he uses DNNs over traditional methods like XGBoost 90% of the time even for tabular data (implying 100% for unstructured data like text, image and sound). This is despite the fact that XGBoost had been reputed to be one of the most winning algos on Kaggle.

In February the RL godfather Richard Sutton posted his observation about the supremacy of raw computational models over more principled rule-based models in his essay “The Bitter Lesson“.

The bitter lesson is based on the historical observations that 1) AI researchers have often tried to build knowledge into their agents, 2) this always helps in the short term, and is personally satisfying to the researcher, but 3) in the long run it plateaus and even inhibits further progress, and 4) breakthrough progress eventually arrives by an opposing approach based on scaling computation by search and learning. The eventual success is tinged with bitterness, and often incompletely digested, because it is success over a favored, human-centric approach. 

What is “bitter” about this is that our fundamental drive and intuition as scientists, engineers and humans is thwarted by the reality of AI performance. We are accustomed to new fields of science maturing from observation, experimentation and finally explainable theories grounded in universal laws. Models arise expressed in concise mathematics, illuminating by increasingly detailed mechanisms and resulting in more accurate predictions. However, unexplainable DNN AI models are increasingly outperforming the traditional statistical explainable models and we are denied the reassuring mathematical narrative that usually distills a complex world into human scale understanding.

Sutton’s “bitter lesson” essay follows a few other notable essays and debates on the subject of statistics vs ML. In 2001 Leo Breiman’s “Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures” argued that statisticians suffer by restricting themselves to the orthodoxy of modeling random variable processes at the expense of practical effectiveness delivered by data-driven machine learning techniques focused more on predictive accuracy. Andrew Gelman’s blog has a long history of discussing this subject as well – illuminating posts on this from 2008 and 2018). Several more good dialogs on the subject: (1) a simplified high-level conceptual distinction, (2) a concise application of the Breiman Data vs Algorithmic Model distinction and (3) a Venn Diagrams gone wild exploration of multiple distinctions.

However, DNN AI models are also reaching their own roadblocks and limitations. To overcome these obstacles AI experts like Geoffrey Hinton (a 2018 ACM Turing Award winner) have suggested that existing DNN approaches alone are self-limiting and may need to be tossed out to force a fundamental rethinking and enable new breakthroughs. Some leading AI researchers are seeking to overcome current DNN AI limitations by augmenting them with parallel advances in material science, quantum computing, neuroscience and, of course, traditional statistical learning methods.

Against the growing conventional wisdom of Sutton, I’ve been spending an inordinate amount of time on statistical learning approaches over the past several years. Partly this is because I already come from a programming and math background and feel the need to build out my third leg of Data Science Unicornism. A bigger motivation for me has been witnessing the growing trend in AI research incorporating traditional statistical techniques to address key limitations of DNN AI today including:

  • Dealing with uncertainty (e.g. Probabilistic Programming)
  • Explainability (e.g. parametric models > black boxes)
  • Few-shot learning (e.g. Bayesian priors)
  • Causality (e.g. Probabilistic Graph Models)
  • Energy Efficiency (e.g. Neuromorphic models grounded in analog/statistical processing)

In sum, AI/ML is a big tent and getting bigger all the time. The gulf between statistics and AI/ML is a real constraint in most situations, but there are tremendous opportunities for those who can synthesize both approaches. In contrast to Jeremy Howard’s AI-first approach (and many practical tutorials online, at conferences and in trade press like O’Reilly), I also strongly recommend students studying statistical and theoretical mathematical approaches where possible. A few of my favorite courses in this vein include Bloomberg’s Foundations of Machine Learning, Richard McElreath’s Rethinking Statistics and CalTech’s Yaser Abu-Mostafa’s CS156 Machine Learning.

My general advice to students is to build a core competency in one of these two camps (statistics/maths or solid CS theory) and then carefully prioritize readdressing your blind spots in the other camp. On top of this, I encourage students to have a domain of expertise outside these two camps as well be it literature, studio arts, political science, economics, or biology. In the end, statistics and programming are simply tools in service in some greater human desire/need.

Ultimately, the biggest leverage and practical outcomes arise from strategically synthesizing theory/data with deep domain expertise around important issues in areas like healthcare, law, education, public policy, etc. Too many academics and enthusiastic young SV startups error on side of theory or technology as a end in and of itself. Too many decision makers in industry lack an formal understanding of or intuition about theory/data that is increasingly required to strategically leverage technology to solve meaningful real-world problems. Despite frequent media man-bite dog stories to the contrary, the long run averages seem to favor clever application of technology at strategic pressure points using deep domain insights – many of which are relatively simple
