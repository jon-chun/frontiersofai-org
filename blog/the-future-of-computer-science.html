The Future of Computer Science

Home » Blog » The Future of Computer Science
Posted by jonchunJuly 2, 2019

By all present indicators the future of Computer Science looks bright. CS majors/minors have doubled/tripled over the last decade, introductory CS courses are at record levels contending for the most popular courses at even top institutions (Harvard, Yale, Princeton) and starting salaries are one of the few bright spots for new grads.

You will find more infographics at Statista

However, I’m less sanguine about the long-term prospects of ‘pure’ programming careers based upon several decades of perspectives from inside university programs, working as a developer, doing startups, managing software development for big and small corporations inside and outside high-tech as well as researching/teaching ML/AI in recent years. As practiced today, software development is incredibly inefficient, error-prone and expensive. There is tremendous economic incentive to address these problems given the cost of programmers, frequent project failures and many lost opportunities. Technology is becoming advanced enough to address some of these issues and is poised to transform programming for most into a general literacy skill like good writing and basic statistical fluency. Only a small group of the most skilled programmers will work on tasks resembling traditional programming like self-driving cars, Android OS or Tensorflow libraries. For the vast majority the value of programming will come in intelligently combining it with other domains of expertise. As technology continues to rapidly mature this integrated approach of embedding ‘programming’ in domains like computational biology, computational economics and the Digital Humanities (encompassing all the Liberal Arts) will disseminate further into areas like public policy, medicine and management.

It’s amazing how progress in tooling and technology just over the last decade has made star 10x programmers today 100x/1000x compared to programmers a generation or two ago. Even scaling back for the edge case that it is, WhatsApp in 2015 is a peek into future trends in software engineering. Using the high-availability Erlang platform with simple, robust and scalable functional programming techniques only 50 skilled engineers were able to build and support 900M users on its social network. That’s 18Million users per engineer! Now image how many engineers it would take to build and support such an social networks a decade ago – 500? ( or two decades ago on a Palm Pilot – 2000?).

The mental error most media, academia and management make is that all programmers are fungible widgets and budget line items that need to be minimized to maximize profit. While never true, this was a more workable assumption for the average programmer a generation or more ago who worked on relatively constrained commodity tasks. There wasn’t that big of a difference in quality/time metrics for programmers implementing a simple linear regression in Fortran or even a Windows 95 GUI for insurance claims using Borland C++. The variability and complexity was on the order of writing a boilerplate press release or compiling a corporate annual report. “Widgetizing” human programmers was a more viable option in those simpler times.

Now technology has become more powerful and complex providing a much longer productivity lever for skilled and knowledgeable software engineers to standout. This is why I typically sifted through 100 resumes for every hire in my last Silicon Valley startup and our company consisted primarily of CalTech CS grads and Theoretical Physics post-docs. A hundred average programmers cannot create innovative, cutting-edge technology that a handful of brilliant engineers can by creatively and skillfully exploiting the latest tools and concepts. This explains much of the apparent paradox of why leading Silicon Valley firms will pay millions for top CS academics while the average salary for programmer have stagnated for several decades.

The software labor market, like many others in our society, is evolving into a winner-take-all marketplace where globalization and technological progress increasingly magnify small differences in performance. The shockingly small development team for the original iPhone/iPad worked 2.5yrs to create products that represent almost 2/3rds of Apple’s 2016 revenue ($265.5b in 2018). In virtually every high-stakes competitive industry like design (Jony Ive/Apple), sports (LaBron/NBA), entertainment (Robert Downey Jr/MCU) and finance (Bridgewater/Hedge Funds) power-law like distributions of incomes arise. The less friction in the marketplace, the more markets aggregate into unassailable monopolies like Internet search (Google), social networks (Facebook) and retail (Amazon). This helps explain why these companies are 3 of the most valuable companies in the world.

As software systems become more complex, pervasive and mission-critical while making increasingly important decisions over human lives, the pressure mounts to resolve this software crisis. Applications like financial risk modeling, automated trading agents and sophisticated personality models on a massive scale are too profitable, competitive, fragile and litigious to put in the hands of anything but the most competent engineering teams. These increasingly intelligent software systems affect the lives of everyone in society from college admissions, job hiring/promotion and credit access/terms to what news you are fed, whom to date and what prices you pay for products/services. These are the highest-end, hardest-core and most remunerative software jobs of the future and places like Silicon Valley scour the world for top talent.

The rise of more formal paradigms like functional programming, type theory and more powerful AI systems are the key to taming complexity while achieving better reliability and continued performance improvements. These approaches also increasingly abstract away underlying complexity as well as consolidate and automate a growing percentage of what is considered programming today. One just has to look at what the Cloud has done to DevOp jobs like System and Network Administrators or what approaches like AutoML and software like Northstar and AutomaticStatistician are doing/will do to the disproportionate time spent by Data Scientists dealing with computational details rather than focusing on top line concerns.

Last month, Mark Cuban made headlines forecasting the transformation of software development into a more creative, collaborative and communicative profession due to AI. Earlier, I came across a similar and more detailed presentation on this subject a week after it was filmed at an April 2019 conference in India about leading-edge software development. The presenter, Chad Fowler, is a developer, entrepreneur, Venture Capitalist and General Manager at Microsoft so he has a well-rounded view into this topic. The details and timing are a bit fuzzy, but he has enough experience and perspective to explain why 29.1% of software developers who fear AI will render their jobs obsolete.

Chris neatly echos own beliefs on the future of software development that guided the design of the Computational Humanities curriculum I developed and describe in an upcoming paper. Even as I’ve been thinking these thoughts for years, the ideas sound somewhat alarmist when originating from outside my own mind. Being both an entrepreneur and venture capitalist, Fowler is trained and highly incentivized to see beyond present conventions. His job is to coldly anticipate the logical consequences of related trends in market demands, technological change (e.g. AI progress) and economic forces which clearly all algn in the same direction.

It should be noted that there are many who predict a rosier outcome for the future of AI-enabled programmers. The most common line seems to be AI will automate the boring parts of jobs enabling humans to become more productive and focus on their complimentary skills of creativity and empathy. They overlook one major economic incentive pushing automation is to reduce costs which increasingly correlates with headcount. Humans generally represent the biggest cost not only in terms of payroll but also benefits, operational overhead, risk, etc. (e.g. recruiting/hiring/retaining, bureaucratic overhead, theft IP or otherwise, leaving for competitors/startups, liability for incompetence/negligence/malice, absenteeism, strikes, etc). Most economists don’t fully address the reality that retraining programs have very poor outcomes and most new jobs are in the poorly paid service sector. Technology, globalization and financialization have created greater wealth and opportunities for those at the top at the expense of (by some metrics) historic and growing inequalities.

The annoyingly disingenuous aspect of these arguments is the assumption that all workers/jobs are fungible and newly unemployed full-stack WebDevs have skills to immediately create comparable economic value writing novels or doing social work. This is not to say that our economy will not create new, better paying, interesting, and possibly even more numerous jobs in the future (that is the topic of another post). The point here is that much of traditional software engineering is increasingly susceptible to automation which will result in fewer highly-skilled programmers being much more productive using increasingly sophisticated methods and intelligent tools – just extrapolate out the power-law divergence in Silicon Valley salaries noted above.

The arrow of technological progress and guiding economic incentives clearly make the automation and abstraction of much current software programming inevitable. Conversely, domains like Government, Healthcare and Higher Education that rely upon skilled knowledge workers and have been resistant to automation have seen rapid cost inflation labeled ‘the cost disease‘. These fields are somewhat unique in resisting economic incentives due to unique cultural, regulatory and political factors. In stark contrast, Software Engineering is one of the most nakedly capitalistic and ruthlessly optimized for profit professions. Automation and efficiencies are eagerly welcomed by decision makers to create better, faster and cheaper products.

For those on the outside or with only limited view into this phenomena it can be difficult to image how powerful the motivation for change is given all these forces reinforcing each other. Software development may be the most complex venture humans have ever conceived. For example, the Manhattan Project lasted 6 years (1939-1945), employed upto 130,000 people and cost US$2b (US$23b in 2018). Google alone currently employs 100,000 people, develops the most sophisticated AI system in history in an ecosystem of over 2b lines of code and has revenues of US$136b (2018). A high-end car today contains multiple computer networks, about 50 microprocessors, 60-100 sensors with a 100 million lines of code and growing. These are 2 extremes but the challenges of development will only grow as software continues to eat more of the world from AI embedded within digestible smart pills to targeting enemy combatants.

The key to connect the other +99% of humanity with our common computational future is to make programming accessible to non-specialists. As machines become more intelligent they increasingly understand humans on our terms rather than forcing humans to learn rather narrow, complex, and often unintuitive programming languages and constructs. Researchers are exploring different paradigms like functional programming and languages that embed mathematically correct proofs. Programming interfaces using NLP, visual blocks and voice controlled systems designed around natural workflows and human psychology open up programming to the masses.

There are many challenges in designing such a open-ended programming interfaces for generalists. Stephen Wolfram’s Wolfram-Alpha computational system embodies one possible way ‘programming’ could be redesigned around human and natural language rather than machines and traditional programming languages. (see video below starting at 2:20)

Note that intellectual challenges still exists under any future programming paradigm. The cognitive burden simply shifts from the overhead of learning narrow implementation and low-level design details to understanding more general computational aspects of your domain problem and how it can be modeled at high-levels of abstraction. Instead of dealing with questions of closures, dangling pointers or O(nlog(n)) performance optimizations one has to more deeply understand the nature of information, statistical manipulations and metrics as well as computational thinking (e.g. decomposition, pattern recognition, pattern generalization and abstraction and algorithm design). This is one of the reasons why I strongly advocate most students to have one or more strengths beyond Computer Science – domain expertise that can drive their use of Computer Science and give in meaning, value and longevity.
