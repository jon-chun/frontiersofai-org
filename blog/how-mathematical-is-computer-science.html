How Mathematical is Computer Science?

Home » Blog » How Mathematical is Computer Science?
Posted by jonchunMarch 8, 2019

tl;dr: This depends up0n the Computer Scientists and the problem domain they work in. It also depends upon your definition of ‘maths‘. In general, not very much high-level math is explicitly used in the majority of traditional programming jobs although basic statistical skills are fundamental t0 the rapidly growing field of data analytics/data science.

First, the term ‘maths‘ needs clarification. In my experience this term breaks down into three broad categories in rough order of relevance to Computer Science applications:

  • Statistics (traditionally considered a specialty outside mathematics)
  • Applied Math
  • Theoretical Math

Statistics, in both the traditional frequentists and Bayesian tradition, is rapidly becoming a fundamental skill akin to writing well. This is driven by the spread of data analytics in areas from health care to hedge funds and from digital humanities to sports analytics. Nearly everyone in modern society from mechanics to doctors to social workers are increasingly becoming knowledge workers manipulating data with computational tools. Data Science is called the trendiest job today and universities from Denison to Berkeley are integrating statistics, computer science, visualization and storytelling across virtually every discipline beyond CS and STEM.

Far less ubiquitous are the tool makers who are often trained in applied math techniques commonly found in traditional Engineering and Physics programs. Core classes in linear algebra, calculus, differential equations and various numerical approaches to modeling complex systems are necessary to understand and use sophisticated software packages like deep neural network or probabilistic programming frameworks. Techniques like Fourier Transforms from Signal Processing and Monte Carlo Methods from Statistical Mechanics can be directly applied to analyze any time series data or to solve/optimize complex probabilistic models across a broad range of disciplines from literature to econometrics.

The division between statistically educated tool users and applied math trained tool makers is somewhat arbitrary. One distinction is that applied math training is generally required to create non-trivial, robust and easily reusable tools in the form of custom analytic pipelines, monster spreadsheets, complex programming libraries or even stand alone applications. Since the renaissance of Deep Neural Networks in 2012 the spotlight in Computer Science has shifted from traditional Software Engineering to Machine Learning resulting in more applied math and statistics is being taken by Computer Science students.

Theoretical Computer Scientist are relatively few in number and usually concentrated in sub-specialties within academic and research centers. Specialization usually starts in graduate school or with a math undergrad. Exposure to abstract mathematical theory such as Finite Fields and Category Theory can provide intuition into particular and/or fundamental aspects of Computer Science like elliptical curve cryptography or concepts behind functional programming. However, with a growing number of sub-specialities to master in the expanding and rapidly evolving field of Computer Science it can be hard to fit these types of theory classes with an undergraduate degree. In practice, most programming is shaped by practical compromises of meeting release dates wherein as much complexity as possible is abstracted away within well-known and tested resources like OpenSSL libraries, tensorflow frameworks or Scala compilers.

Two more things are worth commenting about a very theoretical approach to Computer Science. First, this potential appeal is self-limiting because the vast majority of humanity does not easily think in mathematical abstractions sufficient for this to be a productive path of study. Secondly however, a subset of these higher level of mathematical abstractions do seem to be leaking into elite programming ranks as specialized high-end applications are increasingly peeking under the covers of things like functional programming and high performance computing to efficiently and effectively achieve their ends.

So in sum, the advice I give my students on this subject: learn as much math as you can without losing the focus, interest or curiosity in the big questions you want to analyzing computationally (e.g. the environment, film scripts, etc). Also, do not let your perceptions of math limit your potential to engage with Computer Science – the majority of programmers and projects I’ve worked with in both startups and Fortune 500 companies never explicitly involved much theory from math.
