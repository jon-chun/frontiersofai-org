Guidelines:

This course emphasizes projects as a way to gain knowledge by hands-on experience. The three mini-projects should result in resuable components and knowledge that enable you to efficiently complete the final project centered on your own interests.  Proposals for final projects will be due by completion of the last miniproject and are subject to course instructor(s) approval.

3 Mini-projects

The 3 Mini-projects are sequenced in terms of complexity and will give you the opportunity to consolidate and demonstrate your mastery of (a) data visualization/storytelling, (b) data analytics/interpretation, and (c) data selection, cleaning and design.  Each miniproject will be well structured and documented in the form of public course repository at Tableau Public or github repositories and should serve as a work project in your own personal digital portfolio.

Main Course Project

The Main Project is designed to give you the opportunity to pursue your personal interests using at least one (possibly several) of the many areas of technology while keeping in mind the social issues and ramifications of any computational approach. Ideally, you will use either the non-programming visual or programming tools and techniques to create an interesting digital humanities project poster that incorporates the 3 fundamental skills demonstrated in the miniprojects:  data wrangling, analysis and visualization. The project should be summarized in poster format for inclusion in both the course website as well as the your personal digital portfolio.

Projects will be graded along three dimensions:

  • Humanities-Related: A research question grounded in some field of the humanities that is relatively innovative in nature and combining unique/informative datasets with appropriate technologies.
  • Interesting/Relevant: A research question that raises some interesting social questions, works towards some betterment for humanity or addresses a personal interest or passion.
  • Non-Trivial/Non-Obvious/Correct: Class projects should demonstrate sophistication in integrating both the humanities analysis and the technical application aspects of our course. This is the most frequent problem we’ve seen with student projects (and heavily criticized DH research as well). Projects should be a humanities-first exploration using as sophisticated technology as possible to achieve the most insightful and legitimate findings. Projects should not primarily be an exercise in programming with accompanying analysis that a scholar in the field would find trivial, obvious or wrong. Poorly conceived projects are often interpreted as disrespectful towards and ignorant of the academic traditions/rigors and de-legitimize DH in the eyes of many humanities scholars.

Any particular project doesn’t have to address all three of these metrics, but those that do not should compensate by augmenting the remaining metrics. If projects rely on largely borrowed code we would expect a corresponding compensatory more detailed written analysis and background research. All research project should cite at least 3-5 sources to provide context and support for your project.  This would include links to code/libraries used, prior related work and references to understand the nature of the research question at hand.
