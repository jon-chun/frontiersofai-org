Modeling – Multi-Model Thinking, Genetic Algorithms, Game Theory, Bayesian Probabilistic Thinking, Epidemiology SIR Model and Climate Models, Diverse Experts on Managing the Coronavirus Pandemic

Monday:            Multi-Model Thinking, Genetic Algorithms, Game Theory and Revisiting Bayesian Probabilistic Thinking
Wednesday:       Epidemiology SIR Model, COVID-19 Simulations, Uncertainty and Limitations, and an Overview of Climate Models
Friday:               Mathematical Models Outside Plato’s Cave, Three diverse views on Managing the Coronavirus Pandemic, Long-term Uncertainty and Civil Rights in a Post-Coronavirus World

Current events swirling around the COVID-19 Pandemic synchronize with this week’s topic on Models. This week we utilize the many mental models, tools and skills we have studied to give you a more nuanced, multi-disciplinary and multi-model approach to understanding complex phenomena like epidemiology, climate change and the evolution of trust in societies.

In particular, this course has exposed you to many factors related to modeling including probability and uncertainty, cognitive bias, data science/machine learning and game theory. At this point, you should be able to think much more critically, constructively and creatively about the many challenges, uncertainties and errors inherent in every stage of model development, interpretation and communication. The ability to think critically and intelligently about models and all they entail will improve your ability to observe, understand, predict and act in the larger world.

We end the week with a discussion on the Coronavirus Pandemic models.We hear perspectives, recommendations and critiques from a variety of multi-disciplinary experts including Medical/Data Science, Medical/Economic and Biostatistics/Epidemiology. We revisit the concept of decision making in the face of uncertainty with models based on data that is unknown, rapidly changing and widely varying. Finally, we look past this current pandemic to how models can best inform and guide humanity in the future.

Questions to Consider for Friday’s Discussion:

This week we examine multi-model thinking in the context of the current COVID-19 pandemic. From the various disagreements and perspectives we can see that many factors go into modeling. We assume familiarity with the most prevalent models and arguments and provide a few different multimodal perspectives that you may not have heard. We encourage you to come up with a more nuanced understanding of the situation that takes into account multiple ways of modeling the pandemic, whatever interpretation you may ultimately come to.

  • ‘All models are wrong, but some are useful’: Everyone tends to focus on the word ‘wrong’ in this expression because models are inherently limited and gross simplifications of reality. However, in the current COVID-19 pandemic the definition of ‘useful’ is key to understanding why models are turning out to be several orders of magnitude off. For aviation/aeronautics, useful models prioritize accuracy or planes fall from the sky. For epidemiology, models often take years of data collection/analysis to gain some accuracy (the historical track record is that initial epidemiology models are often very inaccurate). Still, early COVID-19 models are ‘useful’ if they can help optimize decision-making in the face of uncertainty (risk mitigation), persuade leaders to adopt these decisions, and enhance public health policy compliance. How useful do you think the early models were given how “wrong” they were?
  • Bad Data: Modelers are only as good as the data that informs them–a concept captured in the well-known IT/Data Science acronym GIGO or ‘Garbage In, Garbage Out’. This is not an attack on epidemiology models in particular but rather a reflection of the reality that the media, public and politicians are hoping for immediate answers to questions that are impossible to know.  Review the 538 article on the difficulties of modeling the COVID-19 pandemic and make sure you understand which parameters have the largest error and why. To what extent should the public have been made aware of the uncertainty of the models? Is it worth it to ignore this uncertainty in order to ensure better compliance with social distancing? Might we be able to assure the public that models can be very wrong while at the same time instilling a faith in science? 
  • Can we as a society maintain open and healthy debate and constructive criticism around these models without propagating fake news? Should critiques of current models and experts be allowed on social media or should they be censored? How much freedom and which civil rights are you willing to sacrifice so modelers can acquire better data?
  • There is heated debate about the effectiveness of social distancing. What data does Dr. Wittkowski use to argue that it doesn’t work? How persuasive is his argument? Some critics also suggest that the tradeoffs of quarantine are not taking into account other factors (i.e. they are not multimodels). What are these other factors, and how should they be weighed? If COVID-19 were to prove not as deadly as these critics suggest, how will we accept this information and how will help care for those who have lost their jobs due to the quarantine?
  • Error/Confusion Matrix: It’s very difficult to get details on how accurate the tests are. The main test to identify infections seems to be a PCA which detects infections within a limited time window and possibly with only 70% sensitivity. At what level of false negatives do lockdowns become ineffective by releasing infectious individuals back into general society? What would be the consequences of a high false positive rate? What levels should society expect as a reasonable tradeoffs for these tests?
