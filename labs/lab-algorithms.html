Theory of Computation:

Models of computation in CS primarily rest upon the ideas of Turing Machines and Lambda Calculus. Imperative programming paradigms like Procedural and Object-Oriented programming largely trace their lineage to the Turing Machine. The Turing Machine model of computation consists of a global state that is modified by the flow of execution through a sequence of commands (e.g. like following a recipe or driving directions)

Declarative programming paradigms like Functional and Logic programming languages naturally arose out of Lambda Calculus. The Lambda Calculus model of computation is ideally stateless wherein data flows through a sequence of formulas/rules to produce a return value (e.g. like a spreadsheet or SQL). A passing familiarity with these two intellectual traditions helps to understand the fundamental thought patterns embedded in computational thinking and the various problem spaces wherein each would be most appropriate.

We also explore other models for representing computational thought independent from the flows of execution through commands (Imperative) or data through rules (Declarative). Finite State Machines are a state-based style of computation often used to design simple but reliable hardware devices (e.g. vending machines). Some believe that Cellular Automata is the best model for modeling the computation inherent in all matter and physical systems from geological processes to biological cells. From the ancient Greek celestial gear mechanisms to the millions of lines of code in modern cars we see the computational algorithms date back thousands of years and are rapidly proliferating throughout the world today.

Programming Paradigms:

As we'll see, typical computational thought decomposes thinking into unnaturally small discrete steps that do not map seamlessly onto human thinking. Most humans tend to organically think and speak in inexact terms at generally higher levels of abstraction typical of Natural Languages. In contrast, programming languages are Artificial Languages designed to reduce communication to a narrow, unambiguous and efficient mode of expression more akin to mathematics than English.

We demonstrate several programming languages at different levels of abstraction from theoretical Turing Machines to Machine/Assembly to several high-level languages. This is designed to help students more naturally think at multiple levels of abstraction to better bridge the gulf between Natural Languages and Programming Languages that can make programming so difficult at first.

Finally, we introduce and demonstrate several algorithms across very different programming paradigms. This serves several purposes: (a) illustrate the intellectual history and interdependence of artificial intelligence across multiple fields in the Humanities, (b) introduce students to multiple cognitive paradigms to expand their intellectual toolset and outlook, and (c) concretely demonstrating the 'No Free Lunch' Theory by showing how problems impractical under one paradigm can be trivial under another (and visa-versa).

Object-Oriented Programming (@10:24): (Ruby Quickstart)

class CashRegister
attr_reader :drawer

def initialize
@drawer = [2000, 1000,
500, 100,
25, 10,
5, 1]
end

def make_change owed, tendered
difference = tendered - owed

change = []
i = 0
denomination = @drawer[i]

while difference > 0 do
if difference < denomination
i += 1
denomination = @drawer[i]
next
end

change << denomination
difference -= denomination
end

change
end
end

# myCR = CashRegister.new()
# myCR.make_change(437,500)

Functional Programming (@15:25): (Lisp-like Racket at tio.run)

lang racket/base
(define (fact n)
(cond
((<= n 1)
1)
(else
(* n (fact (- n 1))))))
(fact 5)

Racket make-change (@17:00):

lang racket/base
(require racket/list)
(require racket/bool)
(define (make-change x denoms)
(cond
((= x 0)
'())
((empty? denoms)
false)
((< x (car denoms))
(make-change x (cdr denoms)))
(else
(cons (car denoms) (make-change (- x (car denoms)) denoms)))))
(make-change 437 '(500 100 50 25 10 5 1)
