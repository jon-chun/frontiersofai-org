Natural Language Processing – Literary Studies
M Class:             IR and NLP Techniques
W Lab:               Various NLP Libraries
F Discussion:     Humanism and Posthumanism

Of all the kinds of data we look at, unstructured text is one of the most interesting and challenging. From web sites, social media, research papers and published reports, the world is awash in unstructured text awaiting analysis. Even in the massive and rapidly growing video and audio dataset the ground truth is often most readily and accurately extracted from the spoken language component rather than the visual or sound modalities (tone/speed/rhythms, facial expressions, body language, etc). 

Natural Language Processing (NLP) arose from computational approaches to study written human language. Some of the earliest were simplistic word frequency and concordance studies on the questions of author attribution in the late 1800s. With the founding of the field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the decade after WWII spawned the first digital computers, optimism was high. Marvin Minsky argued that computers would soon be translating Russian and many researchers believed natural languages could be captured with human-level abilities using Chomsky Grammars.

Approximately seven decades later these early NLP forecasts there is still no clear path to human-level NLP. Disillusionment from these false promises led to a series of AI winters and the near complete abandonment of NLP approaches prior to the rise of statistical machine learning models and deep neural nets.

The constant stream of impressive breakthroughs like BERT last year and GPT-2 last month are rapidly advancing State of the Art (SOTA) benchmarks for NLP.  Although we are still far from Artificial General Intelligence, one of the best metrics to gauge distance from this watershed event is to track progress within the field of NLP.

Data encoded in text requires a number of special preprocessing steps, some universal and some specific to a diverse set of downstream NLP tasks like sentiment analysis, topic modeling and machine translation.  The latent information, knowledge and wisdom embedded within text at various higher levels of abstraction can be elusive to detect and capture.  NLP approaches to textual analysis, comprehension and generation are still naive in many ways and subject to much skepticism, criticism and human review at this time.

There are arguments that the ambiguity, inconsistency and combinatorial expressiveness of Natural Language may be the key evolutionary advantage in the success of the human species.  Language has enabled humans to encode information, communicate, empathize, cooperate and imagine/create at unparalleled levels.  These same traits render most NLP tasks vastly underspecified and computationally intractable outside narrow tasks under current paradigms and associated technologies.  For all these reasons, NLP is one of the most challenging and fascinating topics we cover this semester.  NLP highlights the limits of Artificial Intelligence, illuminates the essence of human intelligence and highlights the fuzzy and rapidly shifting boundary between the two.

Questions to Consider:

  • How do you feel about the digital humanities as a legitimate academic discipline?  Can technology augment, replace and even supersede our ability to study and understand ourselves within the traditions of the Humanities?  Is your emotional response different from your logical response?  Describe each one in detail.
  • Where do you think the field of Digital Humanities is now and how do you see it evolving over the next decade or so?
  • Which (sub)fields within the Humanities will be the last to be augmented by technology (e.g. studio arts, film, music, literature, poetry, etc)?  Why?  What specific capabilities do you envision that current technology must master to productively contribute and understand these fields?
  • Some complain that computational literary text analysis or distant reading is a mindless statistical parlor trick.  What if AI can analyze text larger sets of text faster, more accurately and with more consistency than humans alone while providing newer more relevant insights than humans?  If important new breakthroughs and understandings are achieved in the Humanities does it make a difference if it was source by machine or human?  Why?
  • What if readers and researchers find AI-generated literature and literary analysis preferable to human-generated versions?  Would you accept AI-based literary analysis on par or superior to human-generated versions?  Why not?
  • Compare Stephen Marche’s article “Literature is not Data” with the response article by Syme and Selisker entitled “In Defense of Data”, both published in the LA Review of Books. Which side of this argument about Digital Humanities did you find more convincing?  What particular arguments within each article did you find the most and least persuasive?  Make a very brief list of your ideas on these issues and come to class ready to share with the class.
