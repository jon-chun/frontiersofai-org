Our third and final Data Analysis Miniproject is due by the end of this week, so we’ll open this week with a review of the 3 popular Natural Language Processing techniques you may choose from for this miniproject (and/or the main class project).  The main objective miniproject #3 is to find an interesting intersection between NLP techniques, a particularly interesting textual dataset and a compelling visual narrative to communicate your findings. 

Regarding this week’s topic of Social Networks, we begin by examining cognitive biases, persuasion techniques and psychological aspects of human cognition.  We’ll view human motivation, irrationality, and malleability from a variety of perspectives including Social Psychology, Behavioral Economics and Cognitive Science. 

Various approaches to hacking dating apps will demonstrate how we can translate this theoretical knowledge about individual and social psychology into models we can build to train, predict and evaluate relationships in the real world. In concert, we’ll consider techniques to capture, model and exploit human emotions and social behavioral patterns using network theory and implemented in social media.  The ultimate goal will be to try to quantify the power of techniques as well as the impact on society from trolling to voting patterns to manufacturing consent/public opinion.

Discussion Questions:

  • What are the top 5 cognitive biases you most frequently fall into the (here in detail if you’re interested)?  Think of specific family and friends and try to predict a list they would confirm.  Which cognitive biases do you suspect would differ between your own self-assessment and those of people who know you best?  Why?
  •  Are there situations, individuals, roles or some combination thereof where your cognitive biases become stronger, more frequent and/or more numerous?  Which are more immutable and which are more variable depending upon context?  Why do you think this?
  • It’s often reported that we are far more accurate judges of the cognitive biases in others than of the biases we have ourselves.  If this is true, can you imagine an evolutionary advantage or other explanation for this asymmetry?
  • Do you believe individuals can overcome their bias?  If so, by what specific means?  If not, what evidence leads you to this conclusion?
  • Benjamin Libet’s neuroscience research demonstrated brain activity directly leading to active decision making may begin 100s of milliseconds before we were conscious of our decision.  Libet’s experiment questions the biological reality of free will and has spawned a cottage industry around physiological experiments into free will.  How do you interpret this experiment as a commentary on conscious free will?  Do you believe free will exists absolutely, only in degrees, varies by situation and decision type, is an illusion, etc?  Support your belief with a clear, logical presentation of evidence/belief.
  • Developmentally, culturally and legally we believe in degrees of free will.  Laws recognize the concept of diminished responsibility.  For example, older children are held to more accountable than their younger siblings.  Morally we tend to judge the consequences for free will by taking into account a wide range of externalities such as upbringing, culture, economic opportunity, IQ, etc.  If technology could more accurately and scientifically quantify the biological basis for degrees of free will should we pursue this and implement technology around such a finding?  How would you balance the dehumanizing effects of such technology against the desire for a transparent, fair and accurate assessment of human free will and consequent responsibility normalized around each person’s unique degree(s) of free will?
  • Evolutionary psychology suggests we developed very fast unconscious heuristics that helped us survive and reproduce in a competitive, dangerous and hostile world.  These unconscious heuristics (e.g. implicit bias) evolved over 10,000s years and once favored our reproductive success but now often lie at the heart of ills of modern society like tribalism/racism.  Other cognitive biases we’ve evolved like high-trust social networks render us easy targets for fake news, demagogues or predatory financial lenders.  Is there an organic way to become self-aware of, moderate or optimize our innate biological cognitive irrationalities?  If there were technology available, would it be ethical to use such measures and in what measure?
  • In contrast to this week’s focus on human cognitive failings/manipulability via technology, last week we saw the simple, transparent and demonstrably logical/fair cognition we can build into robotic AI.  How much, if any, of our messy evolutionary cognitive systems like cognitive biases should we design into our robotic AI?  For example, while in-group bias in humans can lead to racism, it is also lies at the heart of the nuclear family, community and high-trust society – all of which have led to desirable outcomes like better systems of research, justice and economic trade.  How can AI systems design in the benefits of in-group bias without the also producing the frequently toxic fruits of such. Is there a compromise design solution?  You you be willing to live in a redesigned society that eliminates racism, et. al at the cost of stripping you of all your social network benefits (e.g. family wealth and powerful social connections).
  • How programmable do you believe you are?  How about your family and friends?  How about society at large?  The potential threat to society Social Media represents is proportional to the programmability of the masses.  If we’ve established that all humans are programmable to some degree by evolutionary design (and perhaps many much more so than others), how can Social Media best be implemented to avoid abusive use by natural monopolies or authoritarian governments?  On a sliding scale from the free marketplace of ideas all the way to strict government regulation, where do you find your ideal compromise point and why?  Given the economic, political and cultural forces and momentum currently at work, where do you see the historical trajectory heading?  Is this good or bad?  Why? Is there really an option to change course is the future outcome already determined by today’s initial state?
