Data Analytics – Criminal Justice
M Class:            Decision Tree Ensembles and Analytics Pipeline
W Lab:              Kaggle and RapidMiner
F Discussion:    Data: The New Oil or a Civil Right, Free Markets and Regulation

This week we’ll explore the science of constructing models that use data to capture, explain, predict and potentially modify outcomes in the world around us. In particular, we’ll focus on the practice of Data Science to build the arguably most popular models today using the the Python libraries scikit-learn and XGBoost.

Hopefully, you’ll begin to see the coherence in our course as we draw upon many concepts/skills you’ve already studied like Python, data structures, databases and regular expressions. This week will also lay a conceptual basis for future topics which all revolve around building a variety of complex models of text, people, social networks, ro/bots, biology and human intelligence.

The social question this week relates to the growing power, use and accuracy of models over humanity. With ever more powerful hardware, efficient algorithms and massive datasets our models demonstratively outperform humans on a growing range of decisions with broad and/or deep impact.  These data models are increasingly deployed to make/assist decision making for medical diagnosis, loans, college admissions, hiring, criminal sentencing, predictive policing, identifying/killing combatants in war, etc.  What are the limits of this shifting border between human responsibility and machines as subrogates? As accuracy generally correlates to complexity, how can fairness, accuracy and transparency (FAT) be built into our models in the face of powerful economic incentives to automate costly, unreliable, inconsistent and sometimes malicious humans out of the decision-making process? How will the overall trend towards machine models and automation effect economics, law and politics and how to we shape those changes to the benefit of humanity?

Wednesday Lab:

We try to avoid assuming any prior computer science or statistics in our course and only introduce what we can build from first principles where central to a concept.  In the case of Data Analytics as we saw on Monday, Software Engineering and Statistics are central so we’ll delve into a fair bit of more statistics this week than any other this semester.

Given the diversity of the Maths/Stats backgrounds/interests of our class, we provide multiple options for our Wednesday readings: one relatively math-free and one involving math at two levels. This is because students come with a variety of backgrounds. Please try to challenge yourself and reach beyond your comfort zone.  In particular, the videos do an excellent job of explaining and simplifying common statistical ML concepts with animations that are difficult to learn any other way.  You don’t have to grok everything on first viewing, but if persist through these you will have a good foundation to understand and get much more out of the models we’ll review in class.

If statistics is not your thing, this may be your best last shot at rekindling that lost romance. The less you come into this week understanding the more you can potentially gain from these readings if only to improve your intuitions and ability to communicate with technical teammates in the future.

Questions to Consider for Friday’s Discussion:

  • Try to enumerate as many decisions machine learning makes over your life.  For example, credit scores, insurance rates, college admissions, hiring, home loans, etc.
  • For each area you identified above, try to think about how much of the overall decision is based upon machine learning and how much upon human judgement.  For example, credit scores and loan applications tend to be fully determined by machine learning (data + algorithms) while college admissions are likely a hybrid (how much)?
  • Consider current trends and momentum.  Which of the areas you identified above are likely to be increasingly determined by machine learning alone?
  • How do you feel about machine learning increasingly making decisions for you and society at large (e.g. predictive policing and AI-assisted judicial sentencing guidelines).
  • We read a number of articles that raise concerns about bias embedded in the data and algorithms behind machine learning.  What about the bias, irrationality and inconsistency inherent in human judgement?
  • If studies show that one could develop a machine learning system less biased than human judgement, should society hand over all such decisions to machine learning systems?
  • Where machine learning systems make important decisions over humans, there is always a potential for bias and outright fraud to creep in by governments, hackers or private companies who own/control the data/algorithms/systems that run the machine learning systems.  How could society best ensure that such systems are not corrupted either intentionally or unintentionally?
  • As the technology of such machine learning systems becomes increasingly complex, these systems hold the promise of ever improving performance and fairness.  At the same time, the number of people who could accurately inspect and judge the fairness of such systems would shrink.  At what point, if any, should society limit the sophistication and benefits of such machine learning systems to ensure there are a minimal number of people who can judge the fairness and accuracy of such systems?
  • Currently the vast majority of data, algorithms and systems that implement machine learning are held as private property by large corporations and protected as proprietary trade secrets and intellectual property.  In the cases where these machine learning systems are making wide-ranging life decisions over many individuals in society should there be some transparency and/or government regulation?  Where should the line be drawn between private property rights over the machine learning systems and government regulation in the name of individual civil rights?
