Simulations

Over half a century of Moore's Law has enabled powerful new computational approaches. These can create and run simulations of complex models on massive datasets. Unlike models in introductory physics and engineering, models for many interesting phenomena (for example, predicting elections or modeling personality) cannot be reduced to analytical equations with tractable solutions. In such situations, computers allow us to surface key latent relationships that would otherwise be impossible to detect, quantify and model. Even for noisy data riddled with outliers, voids and shifting distributions in the statistical properties of big data (combined with various techniques like regularization and massive new modeling paradigms like deep neural networks) can clarify, extract and model key features, complex relationships and predict the evolution of systems over time.

This week we'll focus on a few important dynamic models that explicitly add the dimension of time to our models from last week: disease outbreaks, human behavior (the evolution of trust), women and promotion. We'll extend the REDCAPE multimodal way of thinking from last week to see how human behavior can be modeled, predicted and changed by technology. This helps lay the foundation for our upcoming week on social networks.

Tuesday starts with a slightly deeper dive into the theory and practice of statistics. We'll also begin our exploration of modeling humans and learn about network models and models of value and power. We'll discuss ways very slight differences in behaviors (i.e. the evaluation of female workers) can lead to very large discrepancies in outcomes. Thursday we'll simulate disease outbreaks. We'll use game theory simulations to witness how trust can slowly spread like a contagion despite clear incentives for short-term betrayals and other immediate self-optimization strategies. We'll also consider the ways Machine Learning might create new and better opportunities to address a problem with large organizations/governments. Miniproject#2 One week from today (November 18th). Possible 3 day extension max. 

The goal of this project is a) to explore the complexity of fitting a model to your text, b) experiment with tuning via smoothing and window size and c) find a non-obvious story in the data. Please put graphs and interpretation with specific references to text/cruxes and peaks in a word doc and upload to Moodle.

Below are questions to answer:

1) Explore at least two DIFFERENT models  (note VADER and TEXTBLOB are often quite similar so don't compare unless they diverge significantly) and decide which of the two works best. Give an example of at least one peak or valley as evidence for this decision. You can look around the line numbers of differences in peaks and valleys to make a quick call (note earlier in the notebook each line of text is given a number--look there). 

2) Explore at least two smoothing algorithms (sma, exponential, savtisky-golay) and give an example of at least one peak or valley to justify why one is better than the other.

3) Explore at least two different hyperparameters  (i.e. default and a different option; rule of thumb: you will want to try smaller for a very large text, larger for a smaller text) for your smoothing method of choice. Again, give an example of why one seems to work better than another using evidence.

REMEMBER points near neutral may not be significant, and models may not do well with the beginning and end of the novel where there is less data to produce a strong signal).

NOTE: in an ideal world this process would be iterative, or you would actually try each model with each smoothing. HOWEVER, here we're just trying to get a sense of differences in models/smoothing algorithms/hyperparameters. 

4) Analyze at least 2 significant peaks and 2 valleys. REMEMBER that the capital letters signify the actual peak or valley, but you are averaging/smoothing the graph, so this is approximate rather than exact. Do the peaks and valleys comport with your predicted graph? Which cruxes did you not predict? What, if anything, does the model miss? 

5) What is the story in your model? What did you notice from the emotional arc that is non-obvious?  (In other words, although all models are wrong, what is useful here for understanding the way the text works?)

For examples of method and analysis feel free to consult Section 2 and Section 3 of The Shapes of Stories. I wrote the book in 2021 and it was published in 2022. Since then we have explored using LLMs and other smoothing methods (like savitsky-golay), which I would encourage you to try if you'd like to use sentiment analysis for a final project. We will have a library with the ability to use an LLM available for your final project if you'd like try to a state-of-the-art approach. I have been very impressed with some of them--they are tend to be more accurate, although even the simpler models often do quite well as long as your text is not overly complex, satirical, ironic, etc.
