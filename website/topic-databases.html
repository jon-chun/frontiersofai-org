Databases - The New Surveillance State

Data has been called the new oil. In only a single generation, oil, gas and auto companies have been displaced by Silicon Valley companies--which collect vast amounts of data--as America's most valuable corporations. When we think of surveillance states, it's easy to think of attempts by former Soviet-bloc countries to gain total information awareness on their citizens by relying on human informants and low-tech bugging devices. Today, however, it's possible to gather this information simply from our technology--cameras on street corners, gps locations emitted by smartphones, and the constant streams of data each of us emit when using everything from browsers to smart cars.

We'll discuss what it means that so much of this data is being collected by private companies with little to no oversight or regulation. We'll also consider the rise of the surveillance state. For some nations, this surveillance is public (i.e. well-known). Sometimes, it's secretive, as Snowden revealed in the case of the U.S. What limitations on surveillance should we expect from a government in a democratic system? Will the greater potential to spy on its citizens be a power that any government will be able to resist? What about the benefits of safety and security that come with such surveillance? What is the argument against "having nothing to hide"?

Two developments make the surveillance state possible: the new mass collection of data, and its storage, retrieval and analysis thanks to ever larger, cheaper and more powerful databases. For this reason we begin with an exploration of the practical concerns of collecting, storing and organizing data in databases. Datacamp will introduce an overview of how to work with relational databases. 

We also consider the ethical implications of the ways internet companies make money off of our data. One of the readings, for example, shows how businesses conceive of both the data we willingly give over and the data that is captured (for example, from our browsing activities) as "valuable." Should we be able to "own" our data like an asset class (i.e. real estate or bonds) and charge for it? Will encryption and blockchain technologies allow us to take back our data? We then move to questions concerning governmental capture of data. Does it matter that the creation of the internet was funded by the U.S. military? Even if some of us feel we have "nothing to hide," how might a surveillance culture challenge fundamental pillars of democracy?
