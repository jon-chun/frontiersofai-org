Models

Perhaps the most important thing to remember about models is the saying by George Box: “All models are wrong. Some models are useful.” All models come with some degree of uncertainty, irreducible error and potential bias. The most powerful state-of-the-art (SOTA) models have been growing exponentially in compute, data, complexity, and energy requirements. But the more complex the model, the greater the risks for inadvertent or intentional harm. 

The increasing size and cost point to real issues of access and transparency. The RoBERTa model, one of ~40 models in the SentimentArcs ensemble you studied last week, has 354 million parameters alone. Deep neural network (DNN) Attention Models similar to GPT-o1 have broken the billion parameter mark and require millions of dollars to train.  Such massive resources are accessible only to the largest corporate and government research groups.

This week we start with an overview of the theory of models. Next we’ll explore the application of some of the most common and simple statistical models, namely linear regression models and classification models. Many disciplines often work with a few key models. In this course, we focus on a multi-model approach in which the first question we ask is “which model?” and “why?” According to the author of the reading this week, core curricula like the IPHS Odyssey course offer some of the first examples of multi-model thinking. Here we continue that approach by updating thought models with computational models.

On Friday we will consider both the theoretical and practical questions surrounding the use of models. On the theoretical level, we’ll consider whether math just allows us to approximate reality through models, or whether “the laws of nature are written in the language of mathematics” per Galileo. Is math our greatest invention–allowing us to predict outcomes and act in the world with agency? Or is it instead just a discovery of the pre-existing fundamental nature of reality?

On a practical level, we’ll look at why COVID-19 has been so hard to model. Given the difficulty, officials have had to make public policy decisions in the face of very real uncertainty. How much of this uncertainty should be conveyed to the public? Is the noble lie necessary in order to ensure compliance with any and all recommendations? How do we treat those who publicly disagree? We’ll also have a small glimpse into the issues of fairness and accuracy that can arise from complex models that use Machine Learning.

  • Test Your Understanding
    • Today’s readings seed our debates about our models.
    • Many argue that the limitations of models (climate, COVID-19, etc) cannot be understood by the general public, and that it is too dangerous to discuss these limitations openly (in essence, a version of the “noble lie.” ) Is it possible to admit uncertainty while maintaining public trust? How much should be shared with the public? Can “we” handle it?
    • Several of the participants in the Stanford roundtable discuss the isolation and animosity they experienced when they raised questions. What level of national emergency (if any) justifies stifling open dissent? Consider the argument that COVID doubled every two days and decisions had to be made quickly. Also that good decisions and good outcomes are not always the same. We also now know that twitter was pressured by government officials to deplatform/censor various viewpoints on COVID. Was this justified?
    • Based on the Stanford roundtable video, classify the kinds of errors made in pandemic policy (i.e. failure to update priors with new information; failure to consider many models, etc. ) How do we balance saving lives, saving livelihoods, mental health issues, etc?
    • Some argue (whether for climate change or COVID) that any model should take into account a diverse set of downstream effects to include disparate impact, economic impact, etc. To what extent (if any) did COVID policy fail to take a many model approach? Which models were excluded, and what were the consequences?
