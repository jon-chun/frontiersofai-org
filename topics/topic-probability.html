The Bayesian statistician Bruno de Finetti (1906–1985) began his book on probability theory with the declaration: “PROBABILITY DOES NOT EXIST.” The capitals appeared in the original, so I imagine de Finetti wanted us to shout this statement. What he meant is that probability is a device for describing uncertainty from the perspective of an observer with limited knowledge; it has no objective reality. 

– Richard McElreath in “Statistical Rethinking“

Making optimal decisions in the face of uncertainty is the focus of this week. Statistics and probabilistic thinking can help us identify the faulty reasoning, bias, and uncertainty inherent in most real-world decisions. Still, if we’re not careful, even statistics can yield pitfalls, as we’ll see with examples like Simpson’s Paradox and a phenomenon called “p-hacking.” You’ve probably also heard the famous adage that correlation doesn’t imply causation, but did you know that sometimes it can? In fact, correlation is a necessary precondition for every causal relationship. Knowing when and how correlation is related to causation is crucial.

Typically, we think of computation as deterministic (i.e. True/False or fixed point solutions) and statistics as frequentist (i.e. probabilities derived from long-run frequencies of events with fixed underlying variables). But this week we explore the more complex worlds of probabilistic programming and Bayesian statistics. These both yield more nuanced models of reality by allowing us to weigh prior knowledge and to update probabilities as new information arises. These programmatic and statistical paradigms also map more naturally to our human understanding of uncertainty, risk, and decision-making.

The larger goal, of course, is to better assess our present and more accurately predict future outcomes to optimize decision-making. This motivates a review of common errors in which statistical thinking can mislead, a deep dive into causation, and a consideration of ways to incorporate Bayesian thinking into our analyses. The current “Replication Crisis ” further complicates a simple deferral to experts or a naive trust in science. The good news is that recent investigations into what makes a “superforecaster” mean that everyone can become better forecasters and decision-makers even in the face of incomplete information.
