Topic: Data Private

Home » Topics » Topic: Data
Created: 2019-01-09 • Modified: 2025-11-17 • Status: private

Data, especially Big Data, has been called the “new oil.” This is why data, along with an intimate understanding of its forms, is the topic of our first week.

We begin by exploring how humans and computers encode data differently. Humans encode data by using perception to store information in memory. For computers, information is usually stored using compression, which we can understand by way of information theory and entropy. In both cases there is “loss,” but for different reasons. Human perception is notoriously fallible, as is human memory. For these reasons, our encoding of data can produce errors attributable to initial perception, encoding, memory storage, retention and recall. For computers, loss is usually an inherent part of the compression process. For this reason, humans and computers, when they are “wrong,” are wrong for very different reasons. And even though we use terms like “memory” for computers, we actually mean something very different.

This technical understanding forms the background for the humanities questions for this week. Philosophers have long debated whether there is a reality beyond sensory data. How do we know what we know, and do we need data, whether sensory or numeric, for knowledge? For Plato, sensory data does not provide access to reality. Therefore, even were we to have a perfect perceptual and encoding process, these together would not lead to wisdom.

If perception can fool us and is inadequate for understanding reality, does “data humanities” provide the solution? To answer this, we need to understand that data can also lead to false conclusions. Data can seem similar when looked at one way and quite different when looked at another (demonstrated by Anscombe’s Quartet). The Monty Hall problem, which we’ll also learn about in class, shows that when it comes to probability, our intuitions are about as faulty as our sensory perceptions can be. For those of you who suspect that data may be no better than perception in accessing the truth, you may find the newest theory of information more appealing. This latest theory proposes that more fundamental than matter is information, a notion we’ll return to next week when we discuss consciousness.

Our discussion will revolve around the question “Are We Data?” We can often be identified by just a few pieces of information, and some researchers even suggest that with 300 likes an algorithm can know you better than yourself. Are we data? What elements of being “you” are unquantifiable? We also consider Dataism, and whether, as Harari suggests, it’s our new god. Does Dataism solve the limitations of more traditional methods of knowing? Harari makes a particularly provocative claim about fighting an “old war.” What’s the story he tells? Do you think he’s right?

DataCamp exercises offer a more traditional way of understanding Data Science, and these courses provide foundational concepts and skills. This week’s Data Science for Everyone introduces many terms that we’ll return to in future weeks–the Internet of Things, Databases, Data Visualization, Descriptive Statistics, Modeling and Forecasting, and A/B Testing, to name just a few. We have a whole course devoted to Machine Learning and Deep Learning–here you’ll get just a brief overview.

Note: I got a lot of questions about all the tiny details I asked about in class Monday. Don’t worry –you don’t need to know them all right away (and we’ll never count in binary again). We’re “eating the elephant” to begin with but will return to many of these details in later weeks (e.g. SQL the week we look at Databases; traditional machine learning versus deep learning the week we look at AI). Just try to absorb as much as you can.
