Data Science - Bias and Fairness
M Class:            Clustering Algorithms and scikit-learn Pipeline
W Lab:              Linear Regression, Decision Trees and Confusion Matrix
F Discussion:    Bias and Fairness in Data and Models

This week we'll explore the science of constructing statistical models that use data to capture, explain, predict and potentially change outcomes in the world around us. Unlike last week's focus on NLP using unstructured textual data, this week we study Data Science which builds computational models based upon structured numeric data. The practice of Data Science is becoming ubiquitous and the Python library scikit-learn is arguably the most popular library for DS.

The social question this week relates to the growing power, use and accuracy of data over humans. With ever more powerful hardware, efficient algorithms and massive datasets, our models demonstratively outperform humans in a growing range of fields with broad and/or deep impact.  These data models are increasingly deployed to make or assist decision making for medical diagnosis, loans, college admissions, hiring, criminal sentencing, predictive policing, and identifying and or killing combatants in war. 

What are the limits of this shifting border between human responsibility and machines capability as our moral and legal subrogates? As accuracy generally correlates to complexity, how can fairness, accuracy and transparency (FAT) be built into our models in the face of powerful economic incentives to automate costly, unreliable, inconsistent and sometimes malicious humans out of the decision-making process? How will the overall trend towards machine models and work automation affect economics, law and politics much less social structure and individual psychology. How to we shape those changes to the benefit of humanity instead of the benefit of fewer and fewer who are increasingly distanced from humanity in terms of wealth, education and socialization?

Questions to Consider for Friday's Discussion:

  • Consider current trends and momentum.  Which of the areas you identified above are likely to be increasingly determined by machine learning alone?
  • How do you feel about machine learning increasingly making for you and for society at large (e.g. predictive policing and AI-assisted judicial sentencing guidelines).
  • We read a number of articles that raise concerns about bias embedded in the data and algorithms behind machine learning.  What about the bias, irrationality and inconsistency inherent in human judgement? Is it possible that machines are still less bias than humans?
  • If studies show that one could develop a machine learning system less biased than human judgement, should society hand over all such decisions to machine learning systems?
  • Where machine learning systems make important decisions over humans, there is always a potential for bias and outright fraud to creep in by governments, hackers or private companies who own/control the data/algorithms/systems that run the machine learning systems.  How could society best ensure that such systems are not corrupted, either intentionally or unintentionally?
  • As the technology of such machine learning systems becomes increasingly complex, these systems hold the promise of ever improving performance and fairness.  At the same time, the number of people who could accurately inspect and judge the fairness of such systems would shrink.  At what point, if any, should society limit the sophistication and benefits of such machine learning systems to ensure there are a minimal number of people who can judge the fairness and accuracy of such systems?
  • Currently the vast majority of data, algorithms and systems that implement machine learning are held as private property by large corporations and protected as proprietary trade secrets and intellectual property.  In the cases where these machine learning systems are making wide-ranging life decisions over many individuals in society, should there be some transparency and/or government regulation?  Where should the line be drawn between private property rights over the machine learning systems and government regulation in the name of individual civil rights?
