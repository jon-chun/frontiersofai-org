Mini-Project Guidelines:

This course emphasizes practical and meaningful hands-on projects as a way to learn our subject matter. 

3 Mini-projects

The 3 Mini-projects are sequenced in terms of complexity and will give you the opportunity to consolidate and demonstrate your mastery of (a) data visualization/storytelling, (b) data wrangling/cleaning and (c) computational textual analytics.  By the end of this course will have a total of four projects--three smaller and one final--for your personal digital portfolio.

#1 Data Visualization Mini Project: 

Using well-prepared datasets you will create an interactive visualization that uses data to tells a compelling narrative.  You can choose between either the simple drag-n-drop interface of Tableau Public or Jupyter Notebooks using Python visualization libraries like matplotlib and seaborn.

#2 Data Wrangling Mini Project:

For this assignment you answer questions about and modify code in this Jupyter Notebook which is a modified version we presented in class that scrapes and analyzes Twitter. Open the Jupyter Notebook above, copy it to your gdrive and work your way through it. Here is a video walking you through the process of completing this Jupyter Notebook for Mini Project #2. Once you are done, save a copy of your updated Notebook with your answers and share a link to it with chunj@kenyon.edu and elkinsk@kenyon.edu.

This Mini-Project will have you formulate a research topic on any current event. You will create an original dataset of tweets and clean it in preparation for analysis which you will also interpret. A common saying is that Data Scientists spend 70-80% of their time collecting, cleaning and combining data (and the other 20-30% complaining about it). Data Wrangling, as it is often called, also entails many choices that influence the research design and interpretation of your data. While this is most true for numeric data (i.e. how to deal with outliers, null values, etc.), this also holds true for how you prepare textual data. Data wrangling thus contributes to your ability to think critically, creatively and collaboratively in a data-driven world.

Sign up for a Twitter developer account to get authorization credentials. With your dev credentials you can use libraries like tweepy to search and download original raw tweets around some current theme like the upcoming election, Covid19, or any other topic people are tweeting about now. Once you get your Twitter developer credentials, test them out by searching the Twitter API with tweepy using this Mini Project #2 Jupyter Notebook.

For the Mini-Project, you need to identify a set of relevent @users, #hashtags and/or search terms related to your theme. Start scraping several times a day for over a period of at least one week to aggregate a decent sized raw tweet dataset. Note the free 'Standard Search API' rate limitations on making automated API requests (max requests within 15 minute window: 180/user or 450/app as of Oct 15 2020). In collecting data, think about what a "representative" dataset would look like and try to make sure you include a range of tweets when analyzing a complex topic.

Once you have collected a week's worth of tweets, you will then clean/extract the data in them into a usable dataset for future analysis. You can borrow code from Jupyter notebooks on Kaggle or Github to find out how to clean up messy Tweets. There are also many Python github repos and tutorials specifically on cleaning Tweets online. You should use at least two Regular Expressions from the Python re library. Your solution should be a well laid-out and documented Jupyter notebook that you share and runs without errors to output/write a file of cleaned tweets in *.csv format. As always, we are available in office hours or by appointment to offer guidance and support.

  #3 Computational Textual Analysis Mini Project:

For this project you apply some algorithms to analyze the tweets you downloaded and cleaned in Mini-Project #2 and visualize the results. Specifically, you'll complete the Twitter Jupyter Notebook we started for Mini-Project #2.

You will use NLP libraries in the previous notebooks to customize various models and hyper-parameters. Your task is to use NER and Sentiment Analysis to analyze, visualize and clearly extract stories from within the Tweet dataset you collected and cleaned for Mini-Project #2. This is an exercise in Experimental Data Analysis (EDA) as you may be required to iteratively explore and more finely filter out your Twitter datasets to bring clear focus on one of the many stories hidden within your data.

Main Course Project

The Main Project is designed to give you the opportunity to focus on your own passions or interests in designing a custom project. The project will be summarized in a digital humanities project poster and should incorporate the three fundamental skills demonstrated in the mini-projects:  data or text wrangling, analysis and visualization. 

Projects will be graded along three dimensions:

  • Creative or Innovative : Projects should not duplicate existing work. In the case of well-mined datasets, the project should apply a new tool or combine it with other datasets to provide fresh insights. Or, the project should apply an existing tool/code to a new dataset.
  • Interesting and Relevant : Projects should focus on some question or problem that would be of interest to a larger audience.
  • Non-Trivial/Non-Obvious/Correct: Projects should use sophisticated technology to achieve insightful and legitimate findings. Projects should not focus on a problem or interpretation that a scholar in the field would find trivial, obvious or wrong. 

Any particular project doesn't have to address all three of these metrics, but those that do not should compensate by augmentation for the remaining metrics. As just one example, if a project relies on largely borrowed code, we would expect a more detailed written analysis and more background research, as well as a detailed interrogation of the tool. All research projects should cite at least 3-5 sources to provide context and support for your project. This would include links to code/libraries used, prior related work, and references that help define the context of the research question at hand.
