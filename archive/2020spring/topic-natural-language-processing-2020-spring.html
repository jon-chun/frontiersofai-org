Natural Language Processing - Literary Studies
M Class:             IR and NLP Techniques
W Lab:               Various NLP Libraries
F Discussion:     Humanism and Posthumanism

Of all the kinds of data we look at, unstructured text is one of the most interesting and challenging. From web sites, social media, research papers and published reports, the world is awash in unstructured text awaiting analysis. Even considering rapidly growing video and audio datasets the ground truth we seek is often most readily accessible and accurately extracted from the spoken language component rather than the visual or audio modalities (tone/speed/rhythms, facial expressions, body language, etc). 

Natural Language Processing (NLP) arose from computational approaches to study written human language. Some of the earliest were simplistic word frequency and concordance studies on the questions of author attribution in the late 1800s. The first commercial digital computers spawned by WW2 and the field of Artificial Intelligence followed a decade later created tremendous optimism about NLP. Marvin Minsky claimed computers would master natural language within a few years and many researchers believed with human-level abilities were possible using the rules of Chomsky Grammars.

Approximately seven decades after these early NLP forecasts there is still no clear path to human-level NLP. Disillusionment from these unfulfilled promises led to a series of AI winters. Outside narrowly fixed domains, deterministic Chomsky grammars have been abandoned in favor of far more successful statistical machine learning models and deep neural nets. Nonetheless, today true NLP capability is almost synonymous with Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) - a goal without a clear path that remains an unknown distance into the future.

This week introduces new concepts that will apply and extend previous topics introduced to give you an overview of Natural Language Processing (NLP). There will be several short hands-on programming tasks that we'll review in class and build upon in future weeks as well. You'll learn how we represent text as data and use popular Python NLP libraries like NLTK, spaCy and gensim to achieve common tasks like identifying Parts of Speech (POS), diagramming sentences (Dependency Parsing) and doing sentiment analysis.

Sample Questions to Consider for your Discussion Posts:

  • How do you feel about the Digital Humanities as a legitimate academic discipline?  Can technology augment, replace and even supersede our ability to study and understand ourselves within the traditions of the Humanities?  Is your emotional response different from your logical response?  Describe each  in detail.
  • Some complain that computational literary text analysis or distant reading is a mindless statistical parlor trick.  What if AI can analyze larger sets of text faster, more accurately and with more consistency than humans alone while providing newer more relevant insights than humans?  If important new breakthroughs and understandings are achieved in the humanities, does it make a difference if it was sourced by machine or human?  Why?
  • Compare Stephen Marche's article "Literature is not Data" with the response article by Syme and Selisker entitled "In Defense of Data," both published in the LA Review of Books. Which side of this argument about Digital Humanities did you find more convincing?  What particular arguments within each article did you find the most and least persuasive?
