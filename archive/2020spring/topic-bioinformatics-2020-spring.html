If we view humans from a computational perspective, we can see living cells as information processing systems executing DNA code to create proteins within an unimaginably complex biochemical operating system. At the most fundamental level, we are built from a massively parallel computational system based upon carbon and chemistry rather than silicon and electromagnetism operating at multiple levels often conceptualized within a multi-omics framework.

In our antipenultimate week we explore this computational interpretation of biology and life as an information processing system. We'll understand why a computational model of cellular life is orders of magnitude more complex than any computational or mathematical model we've seen so far. The information, behaviors, processing pathways and feedbacks within overlapping and interconnected systems create constantly shifting and dynamically self-modifying networks. These networks are embedded within each other, interoperate alongside each other and simultaneously run with different signaling at multiple scales.

We also see how the unique challenges of bioinformatics require high-performance computational and large-scale statistical methods to study genomics--one of the original big data application (along with astrophysics and experimental subatomic physics). Bioinformatics and computational biology have revolutionized our understanding of genetics, medicine and life thanks in large part to rapid advances in computer science: big data, computational power and improved algorithms. 

As in other weeks, we see how rapid progress in technology is poised to challenge the very nature of humanity.  Forty years ago test tube baby technologies like IVF were new, controversial and pushed the limits of medical ethics at the time. Despite initial attempts to limit access to the procedure, today approximately 10% of all births in Denmark are born with assisted reproductive technology like IVF.

The fictional world depicted in the 1997 film Gattaca is fast becoming a reality.  For the cost of a few thousand dollars, parents can practice statistical 'soft' eugenics by fertilizing multiple eggs, testing each for desirable traits across a million SNPs, and selecting only the 'best' one for implantation. Friday's Economist article suggested initial trails started year 2020 and we'll update the status of this rapdily evolving field.  How can society prevent this resulting in a massive, permanent, and accelerating biological inequality tantamount to a man-made speciation event that irreversibly separating the 'haves' from the 'have nots'?

This week also raises a number of questions about genetic engineering, equitable access to healthcare, immortality, the Singularity and bioterrorism. For those interested, there is optional material above (cut for time) that extends this week's material in the domains of drug trials, pharmacogentics and precision medicine.
