Bioinformatics - Genetics and Molecular Biology
M Class:            Computational Biology, Molecular Biology and Genetics
W Lab:                Drug Discovery and Designer Proteins
F Discussion:    Eugenics, Transhumanism and Immortality

In our second week on Algorithms we noted the computational perspective of living cells as information processing systems. In essence, we are physical computational systems based upon carbon and chemistry rather than silicon and electromagnetism. 

In our penultimate week we more directly explore this computational interpretation of cellular life as information processing systems via the forms, functions and interactions of DNA, mRNA and proteins.  We'll understand why a computational model of cellular life is orders of magnitude more complex than any computational or mathematical models we've seen so far. The information, behaviors and feedbacks between just these three elements alone account for practically uncountable levels of constantly shifting, overlapping, interacting and dynamically self-modifying networks.

We also see how the unique challenges of bioinformatics require high-performance computational and large-scale statistical methods to study various -omics - perhaps the original big data application along with high-energy particle physics (c.f. LHC).  In particular, we'll study the inherently high-dimensional problem of protein folding and learn approaches to this seemingly intractable problem. Progress in this area holds immense potential in drug discovery, gene therapy and personalized medicine as well as in growing crop yields, securing clean water and fighting climate change.

Along with AI and Neuroscience, Genomics is one of the rapidly evolving fields today and all three overlap with the core content of our course. All these fields have been revolutionized by rapid advances in big data, computational power and improved algorithms. In this class we explore how Neuroscience and AI both illuminate key aspects of humanity related to perception, language, intelligence, identity, sociability, economics, bias, ethics, consciousness, etc. This week we'll see how Genomics explains the scaffolding that underlies all these higher order levels of humanity as well as the very foundations of our physical existence.

As in other weeks, we see how rapid progress in technology is poised to challenge the very nature of humanity.  Forty years ago test tube babies technologies like IVF were new and controversial. Despite previous attempts to limit access to the procedure, today approximately 10% of all births in Denmark are born with assisted reproductive technology like IVF.

Today, for the cost of a few thousand dollars, mothers can practice statistical eugenics by fertilizing multiple eggs, testing each for desirable traits, and only implanting the 'best' one.  Last year Chinese researcher He Jiankui applied established CRISPR procedures to edit the genes of twin baby girls making them and their descendants resistant to HIV. This was not a technical breakthrough but rather a crossing of the ethical rubicon in performing these precision genetic edits on heritable human germline cells. How can humanity deal with what seems the inevitable opening of this potential Pandora's Box of eugenics and the consequences that could arise from it like massive, permanent and accelerating inequality?

In addition to genetic engineering, we'll also touch upon areas of drug discovery, systems biology, anti-aging and the Transhumanism / Singularity movement. Given the potential diversity of backgrounds and topics this week we'll again rely upon a variety of shorter videos to capture the broad gestalt of specific topics as well as demonstrate the important connections between them.

Videos and animations are perhaps the ideal format to quickly intuit many of the complicated and interrelated concepts and processes we explore this week. Despite my best screening, inevitably there will be some specialized terminology/concepts in parts of these videos, so focus on capturing the overall concepts and interrelationships rather than details. Practice the valuable skill of reading just beyond the edge of you comprehension/comfort level and eliding over details to grasp the bigger picture of these videos (intellectual grit).

NOTE: We'll cover the essentials of cellular biology, genetics and proteonomics that you need to know in class Monday.  Unfortunately, ours is such an unusual course that I could not find a concise summary of these three topics within a single, well-explained and tractable video of reasonable length speaking at a level of abstraction appropriate for our class.

You are not expected to memorize nor master the many detailed bio concepts. Friday's Quiz will focus on a balance between the general big ideas of selected readings (e.g. the Central Dogma of Molecular Biology representing information flow) yet specific enough that they cannot be guessed without doing the reading. Do not stress over the details and feel free to put a 'black box' around the points in the readings that go too far into the weeds
