Voyager Golden Record (1977)

Week One:  Data (August 28, 30, Sep 1) Lecture:           Can Data Model Our Humanity?        

This week's readings examine the nature of data from two different theoretical perspectives (Philosophy of Information and Theoretical Physics) as well as two more practical perspectives (Information Science and Experimental Physics).  Epistemology tells us the nature, source and limits of knowledge and we compare Rationalism and Empiricism approaches to what our senses and data can tell us.  Finally, our main reading by John Cheney-Lippold explores several real-world scenarios where data fails to encapsulate our humanity (in his opinion).

 

 

Antikythera Mechanism (150-100BC)

Week Two:  Algorithms  (Sep 4, 6, 8) Lecture:           Can Machines Think?        

We begin with a reading that gives a quick overview of humanity's historical journey towards mechanical thinking from the abacus to Aristotelian Logic to Mathematical Logic to Artificial Neural Nets in Cognitive Science.  Next, we get an overview of computer algorithms, applications and asymptotic analysis (efficiency).

We then look at the psychology of human cognition, fast heuristics vs accurate algorithms and cognitive biases that lead us to false conclusions.  Next, we learn a few terms of art regarding human cognition.  This enables us to better understand one neuroscientist's mathematical model of how the human brain can realize a Theory of the Mind as well as explain pathological conditions like Schizophrenia and Autism.

 

 


The Golden Ratio

 

Week Three:  Visualizations  (Sep 11, 13, 15)
Lecture:           Data Journalism and Fake News

This week we learn fundamental design practices of data visualizations and how to summarize, organize and visualize information in a variety of ways within this context.  In particular, we'll explore best practices for designing charts, infographics and UXs (User Interfaces).

Data Visualizations and Descriptive Statistics lie at the heart of Data Journalism.  We'll study both these subjects and how they have been transforming journalism.  In addition, we'll study the art of storytelling within the context of data visualization and learn how stories make visualizations more effective and persuasive.

Finally, we'll cover some of the history, current state and architecture of "Fake News" particularly as it relates to data-driven research and journalism.

 

Ecological Network

 

Week Four:  Operating Systems and Networks  (Sep 18, 20, 22)
Lecture:           Weaponizing Data

In the first three weeks, we studied prototypical examples of the three main components of a computing system:  Input(Data) -> Processing(Algorithms) -> Output(Visualizations).  This week, we'll combine and encapsulate these three concepts into a single abstraction we'll call a "Process".

Furthermore, we'll take this concept of an abstract Process and make it more concrete by seeing how it is realized and executed on a physical computer under the control and coordination of an Operating System (OS).  We'll see how both Processes and OSes are evolving across both physical and virtual devices via virtualization and networking to create the fabric of the Cloud and build massively parallel computational systems.

Finally, we'll use our knowledge of Operating Systems and Networking to explore technological threats in the areas of the Darknet, Viruses/Malware and Cyber Warfare.  In particular, we'll do a forensic examination of the Stuxnet worm and explore Wikileaks Vault 7 to acquire a more informed and critical opinion of the nature and extent of such threats represent to society.

The Dangers of Trusting Robots (BBC.co.uk)

 

Week Six:  Bots (Oct 2, 4, October Break) Lecture:           Automation:  Life of Leisure: Utopia or Dystopia?

This week we build directly upon the last two weeks' topics of networks and programming by studying bots and the Internet of Things (IoT).  Bots are the first technology we encounter that are directly engineered to automate the work of humans.  We'll explore the related issue of possible long-term structural unemployment as well as some potential solutions such as Universal Basic Income.

Data and the Law.  Open DataSets.  Relational Database Management Systems.  NoSQL and other non-traditional database technologies.  Structured Query Language (SQL).  Regular Expression (RegEx).  Remote network requests via REST and JSON APIs.

 

Data Analytics

 

Week Eight: Data Analytics (Oct 23, 25, 27)
Lecture:          Data as a Civil Right

This week we'll learn how we take data and create knowledge using Data Analytic techniques.  We'll collect data from various sources such as government, Kaggle and APIs from sources like the NYTimes.com, Facebook.com and Twitter.  Using data science tools built into the Unix command line, Jupyter Notebooks and Microsoft Azure cloud computing we'll learn the various steps of Data Analytics which mirror the Scientific Method we introduced at the beginning of our course.

This week technical component will be more practical hands-on and less theory, especially compared to last week.  The social issue we discuss on Friday is data as a civil right where we'll see how data is being used and how it potentially impacts our rights as individuals and subgroups within society.

 

Natural Language Processing

 

Week Nine: Natural Language Processing (Oct 9, 11, 13) Lecture:        A Critique of Digital Humanities

Data Acquisition from Open Datasets to Web Scraping to Optical Character Recognition (OCR).  Mathematically representing and algorithmically manipulating text.  Natural Language Processing and the Python NLTK Toolkit.  Textual analysis with R and the tidyverse libraries.  AI-generated text.

 

Spatial Data and Visualization

 

Week Ten: Mapping (Oct 30, Nov 1, 3) Lecture:           Data Regulations

A brief overview of HTML, JavaScript and the Document Object Model will give us the foundation to explore GoogleMaps and the most popular open-source mobile mapping library leaflet.js.  Next, we'll use our API knowledge to acquire geographical climate data, optionally filter it with RegEx and map it in ArcGIS and/or Tableau.

We'll also discuss the particular case of predictive policing which relies on both data analytics and mapping.  More broadly, we'll look at the problems of community, corporate and government regulation of big data and algorithms as potential solutions to the problems of transparency and accountability in balance with freedom of speech concerns.

 

Humanizing Robotics

 

Week Eleven: Robotics (Nov 6, 8, 10) Lecture:           The Ethics of Technology

Robotics sensors, actuators, systems and frameworks including Legomindstorms eV3 and Robot Operating System (ROS).  We'll dive into the generalized computer science principles of robotic frameworks like messaging, channels and real-time vs not-real time OS.

We'll also look at applications of robotics in the military, self-driving cars, manufacturing and healthcare.  Beyond the well-known application specific domains of robotics, we'll explore some of the more cutting edge applications in developing more generalized robotics systems and tailoring human-robotic interactions by programming emotional intelligence into robotic systems.

Finally, we'll examine some of the ethical questions around the applications of robotics as well as some attempts by researchers and organizations like IEEE to define, anticipate and regulate some of the more dangerous aspects of robotics technology.

Week Twelve: Social Networks (Nov 13, 15, 17) Lecture:           The Social Animal Online

This week we'll examine cognitive biases, persuasion techniques and psychology that are part of human cognition.  We'll view human manipulability from a variety of perspectives including Social Psychology, Behavioral Economics and Cognitive Science.  We'll look at techniques to exploit human emotions and behavioral patterns used by social media.  We'll analyze data from Twitter and/or Facebook social research with via Python APIs and GraphX.

Discussion Questions:

  • What are the top 5 cognitive biases you suspect you have?  Think of specific family and friends you have and try to assess as best you can if they would confirm your list.  If not, which cognitive biases would differ between your own self-assessment and those of people who know you best?

  •  Are there situations, individuals, roles or some combination thereof where your cognitive biases become stronger, more frequent and/or more numerous?  Which are more immutable and which are more variable?  Why do you think this?

  • It's often reported that we are far more accurate judges of the cognitive biases of others than of those biases we have.  If this is true, can you imagine an evolutionary advantage to this asymmetry?

  • Do you believe individuals can overcome their bias?  If so, by what specific means?  If not, what evidence leads you to this conclusion?

  • Benjamin Libet's neuroscience research demonstrated brain activity directly leading to active decision making 100s of milliseconds before we were conscious of our decision questioning the biological reality of free will.  How do you interpret this experiment as a commentary on conscious free will?  Do you believe free will exists absolutely, only in degrees, varies by situation and decision type, is an illusion, etc?  Support your belief with a clear, logical presentation of evidence/belief.

  • Developmentally, culturally and legally we believe in degrees of free will.  Laws recognize the concept of diminished responsibility.  For example, older children are held to more accountable than their younger siblings.  Morally we tend to judge the consequences for free will by taking into account a wide range of externalities such as upbringing, culture, economic opportunity, IQ, etc.  If technology could more accurately and scientifically quantify the biological basis for degrees of free will should we pursue this and implement technology?  How would you balance the dehumanizing effects of such technology against the desire for a transparent, fair and accurate assessment of human free will and consequent responsibility?

  • Evolutionary psychology suggests we developed very fast unconscious heuristics that helped us survive and reproduce in a competitive, dangerous and hostile world.  These unconscious heuristics (e.g. implicit bias) evolved over 10,000s years and once favored our reproductive success but now often lie at the heart of ills of modern society like racism.  Other cognitive biases we've evolved render us easy targets for fake news, demagogues or predatory financial lenders.  Is there an organic way to become self-aware of, moderate or optimize our innate biological cognitive irrationalities?  If there were technology available, would it be ethical to use such measures and in what measure?

  • In contrast to this week's focus on human cognitive failings/manipulability via technology, last week we saw the simple, transparent and demonstrably logical/fair cognition we can build into robotic AI.  How much, if any, of our messy evolutionary cognitive systems like cognitive biases should we design into our robotic AI?  For example, while in-group bias in humans can lead to racism, it is also the foundation of the nuclear family and community.  Should robotic AI be completely devoid of the concept of family and community or is there a compromise design solution?

  • How programmable do you believe you are?  How about your family and friends?  How about society at large?  The potential threat to society Social Media represents is proportional to the programmability of the masses.  If we've established that all humans are programmable to some degree by evolutionary design (and perhaps many much more so than others), how can Social Media best be implemented to avoid abusive use by natural monopolies or authoritarian governments?  On a sliding scale from the free marketplace of ideas all the way to strict government regulation, where do you find your ideal compromise point and why?  Given the economic, political and cultural forces and momentums currently at work, where do you see the historical trajectory heading?  Is this good or bad?  Why?
